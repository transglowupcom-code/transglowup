Safe Binding: What to Know First

Binding can help your chest look flatter and help you feel more like yourself. This guide walks you through how to do it as safely as possible, step by step.

November 6, 2026 · 5 min read

What binding is, and how a binder works

Binding means using a specially made compression garment to flatten the appearance of chest tissue, so your chest looks flatter under your clothes. For many people, binding eases chest dysphoria, helps them feel more comfortable in public, and helps other people read them the way they want to be read. Anyone can bind, whatever your gender, and you do not need anyone else's permission to want a flatter-looking chest. A binder is not magic, and it is not meant to make your chest disappear completely. It is meant to compress tissue in a controlled, even way, so the shape under a shirt looks smoother and more neutral. That is an important difference from wrapping something tight around your chest, because a binder is designed for this job: it spreads pressure across the chest, back, and sides instead of squeezing one spot, and it is made from fabric that stretches and breathes as you move and breathe.

A good binder should feel like a firm hug, not like a squeeze that steals your breath. You should be able to take a full, deep breath in and let it out without pain, and you should be able to move your arms and shoulders without sharp pulling. If you cannot breathe deeply, if your ribs ache, or if you feel dizzy, the binder is too tight or the wrong fit, and you should take it off. That rule matters more than how flat you look in the mirror. Your ribs, lungs, back, and skin all have to live with the binder for hours at a time, so comfort and breathing come first, every single time. Binding done carefully is generally considered safe, but binding done too tightly, for too long, or with the wrong materials can cause real problems, including back and chest pain, shortness of breath, skin irritation, overheating, and, less often, rib bruising or fractures. The good news is that most of those problems are preventable, and this guide covers the habits that help you avoid them.

Getting the right size

Size is the single most important safety choice you will make, and it is worth taking your time over. Do not guess your size from a bra size or a T-shirt size, because binder sizing works differently from both. Every maker has its own sizing chart, so use the chart for the exact binder you are buying, and measure yourself the way that maker tells you to. You will usually need a soft measuring tape, and you will measure around your chest at the fullest point and under your chest, standing relaxed, with the tape level and snug but not pulled tight. Write the numbers down, compare them with the chart, and if you fall between two sizes, choose the larger size. A binder that is a little roomier will still flatten your chest, and it will be far kinder to your body than one that is too small.

It is tempting to size down, because it feels logical that tighter must mean flatter. It does not work that way. A binder that is too small usually does not make you look much flatter, and it does put extra pressure on your back and ribs, makes breathing harder, and can leave sore spots, red marks, and bruising where the fabric digs in. If your binder leaves deep marks, rolls up, pinches under your arms, or makes it hard to get a full breath, it is the wrong size, not a sign that you need to push through it. If you have a larger chest, the same rules apply, and you may need extra breaks and a maker that offers larger sizes or made-to-measure options. If you have asthma, scoliosis, a breathing condition, chronic pain, or any other health condition that affects your chest, back, or breathing, talk with a clinician you trust before you start binding, and ask what would be safest for your body in particular.

How long to wear one, and when to take breaks

Keep binding to less than eight hours a day. That eight-hour limit is the high end, not a goal to hit every day, and shorter is better whenever you can manage it. If you are new to binding, start with just an hour or two at a time and build up slowly, taking the binder off for at least an hour in between so you can stretch and breathe deeply. Try to build breaks into your day, even short ones: take it off when you get home, change into a sports bra or a loose top for the evening, and give yourself whole days off when you can. Your body recovers during those breaks, and the breaks are part of binding safely, not a failure to bind properly.

Never sleep in a binder. While you sleep, your breathing becomes shallower and you cannot notice pain or tightness the way you can while you are awake, so binding overnight puts extra strain on your chest and lungs without you realising it. Take it off before bed, every night, no exceptions. Also take it off before you exercise hard or do heavy physical work. A binder is not built to move with you the way a sports bra is, and it can make movement and breathing much harder when your body needs more air. For a workout, a run, or a physically demanding shift, switch to a sports bra or a compression top made for activity. Only use a binder for swimming or exercise if it is specifically made and marketed for that kind of activity, and even then, listen to your body and stop if you feel pain, dizziness, or trouble breathing. Never wear two binders at once, and never layer a binder over another binder or a tight sports bra to get flatter. That extra compression can damage your ribs and lungs and cut into your skin, and it rarely gives you the smoother result you were hoping for.

Warning signs: take it off and listen to your body

Pain is always a signal, not something to push through. Take your binder off right away if you notice chest pain, rib pain, back pain that feels sharp or new, trouble breathing or shortness of breath, wheezing or a rattling sound when you breathe, dizziness, numbness or tingling, overheating, or skin that is sore, broken, blistered, or very red. Stretch gently, take slow deep breaths, and give your body a rest. If pain, wheezing, or trouble breathing does not settle quickly after you take the binder off, or if you feel very unwell, get medical care promptly and tell the clinician that you bind, so they can help you properly. You deserve care without judgement, and a good clinician will focus on helping your body feel better.

What never to use

Never bind with duct tape, packing tape, sports tape, plastic wrap, or elastic bandages such as Ace bandages. This is not a small detail, and it is not about being fussy. Tape that is not made for skin can tear skin, cause blisters and sores, and hurt badly when you take it off. Elastic bandages are designed to constrict, so as you breathe in and out, they get tighter and tighter around your chest. That can restrict your breathing, cut off circulation, and injure your ribs. Plastic wrap does not breathe at all, so it traps heat and sweat against your skin. None of these materials spread pressure the way a binder does, and none of them are safe substitutes, even for a short time or just once. If a binder is out of reach right now, the safer choice is a gentler option from the next section, not a household material that was never meant for your chest.

The same thinking applies to layering lots of tight sports bras or wrapping a scarf or belt around your chest. Those tricks can feel like a quick fix, but they press unevenly, ride up, and make it harder to breathe, and they do not protect your ribs the way a purpose-made binder does. If you are in a situation where you cannot get a binder safely or afford one right now, community binder programs and LGBTQ+ health organisations sometimes offer free or low-cost binders, and a local affirming clinic may know what is available near you. You can ask about that through the Find Care directory on this site, and you do not need to explain your whole story to ask a simple question.

Looking after your skin and your binder

Your skin works hard under a binder, especially in warm weather or if you sweat a lot, so a little daily care goes a long way. Keep the skin on your chest clean and dry, and wash it gently each day. If you notice redness, itching, or a rash, give your skin a break from binding if you can, keep the area clean with gentle soap and water, and watch for signs that it is getting worse, such as broken skin, swelling, or discharge. Some people find it helps to wear a thin, light shirt underneath the binder, or to use a little baby powder or corn starch on the skin to reduce sweat and friction, but stop using anything that irritates your skin, and do not put creams or powders on broken skin. Stay cool and drink water while you bind, especially on hot days, and change out of a sweaty binder when you can rather than sitting in damp fabric for hours.

Look after the binder itself, too. Wash it regularly, because sweat, germs, and odour build up in the fabric and can irritate your skin and wear the binder out faster. Hand washing with cold or warm water and a little soap or laundry detergent, then hanging it up to air dry, is the gentlest way to care for it. Avoid putting it in a hot dryer, using hot water, bleach, or an iron, because heat can damage the fabric and change the fit. If you wash it in the evening and leave it out overnight, it should be ready to wear in the morning. Having two binders, if you can manage it, makes this much easier, because you always have a clean, dry one while the other is washing.

Gentler alternatives for days when binding is not right

Some days, binding is not the best choice, and that is completely okay. You still have options that can help you feel more comfortable. A firm sports bra or a compression sports top can give you a smoother shape with less pressure than a binder, and it moves with you, so it is a better choice for exercise, hot weather, or a day when your chest or back already hurts. Layering can help a lot, too: an open button-up shirt, a jacket, a hoodie, or a patterned top over a sports bra can soften the shape of your chest without any compression at all. Darker colours and thicker fabrics tend to hide shape more than thin, light, or clingy ones, and a shirt with a chest pocket or a bold print can draw the eye away from your chest.

Good posture can also make a difference, and it is kinder to your back than hunching to hide your chest. Standing tall with your shoulders relaxed will usually look and feel better than curling forward, even if curling forward feels protective in the moment. If you are heading into top surgery one day, gentle binding now can also matter for later: binding too tightly for a long time can change skin elasticity and make surgery harder, so taking care of your chest now is also taking care of your future self. Whatever you choose, you are allowed to change your mind from day to day. Some days you may bind, some days you may wear a sports bra, and some days you may not bind at all. All of those choices are valid, and none of them make you any less you.

A simple routine to start with

Measure carefully and choose your true size, or the larger size if you are between sizes. Start with short wear times and build up slowly. Keep it under eight hours, take breaks, never sleep or exercise hard in it, and take it off the moment it hurts. Wash your skin and your binder regularly, and keep a gentler option ready for rest days. If something feels wrong, trust that feeling and ask a clinician for help.
