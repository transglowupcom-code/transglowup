Finding Affirming Care Near You

Good care should respect who you are, use your name and pronouns, and listen to what you need. This guide helps you find a provider who does that — and spot one who does not.

November 6, 2026 · 5 min read

What "affirming care" means

Affirming care is care that respects your identity, your choices, and your health goals, and treats you as a whole person, not as a problem to be solved. In practice, that means a provider who uses your chosen name and pronouns, who asks about your health in a way that fits your body and your life, and who works with you to build a care plan centred on your goals. It means the front desk, the nurses, and the clinician are all on the same page, so you do not have to correct someone new at every step. It means intake forms that ask about your sexual orientation and gender identity in a respectful way, and give you space to share what you want them to know. And it means care that is informed by evidence and experience with trans and gender-diverse people, so your provider understands how hormone therapy, surgery, and other parts of transition can affect your health, and does not treat your identity as a mystery or a surprise.

Affirming care is not only about gender-affirming medical care, like hormone therapy or surgery referrals, although those are important parts of it for many people. It is also about everyday care: a primary care visit, a check-up, a flu shot, help with mental health, reproductive health, or a sore throat. You deserve to feel safe and respected in all of those visits, not only in specialist clinics. A good affirming provider will explain why an exam or a test is needed, ask for your consent before proceeding, and listen when you say you need a pause, a different approach, or more information. They will not make assumptions about your body, your relationships, or your sex life based on how you look or how you identify, and they will ask about your actual health needs and risks instead. If you have ever left an appointment feeling small, dismissed, or misgendered, you know how much difference a truly affirming provider can make, and you deserve that difference every time.

How to use a provider directory

A provider directory is a list of clinicians and clinics that say they offer LGBTQ+ or trans-affirming care, and it is a good place to start your search. TransGlowUp's own Find Care directory, at the bottom of this page, lists verified clinicians, so you can search by location and see what services each one offers. Other trusted directories, such as the GLMA provider directory, the WPATH provider directory, and OutCare Health, also list providers who say they are committed to LGBTQ+ care. A listing is a helpful starting point, but it is not a guarantee, so use the directory to make a short list, and then check each provider for yourself before you book.

Start by thinking about what you need. Are you looking for a primary care clinician, hormone therapy, mental health support, reproductive health, surgery, or something else? Do you need a provider near your home, or would telehealth work for you? Do you need someone who speaks your language, offers evening appointments, or has a sliding scale if you do not have insurance? Write those needs down, then search the directory for providers who list the services you need. Look at their profile or website, and see whether they mention LGBTQ+ or trans care explicitly, whether they list pronouns, and whether they describe their experience with trans patients. Ask friends, community groups, or a local LGBTQ+ centre for recommendations, too, because a personal recommendation from someone who has actually seen a provider can tell you more than a listing alone. Make a short list of two or three names, so you have a back-up if the first one is not taking new patients or does not feel right.

What to check before you book

Before you book, take a few minutes to check the basics, so you do not waste a trip or get a surprise bill. First, check what services the provider actually offers. A clinic that lists gender-affirming care may offer hormone therapy but not surgery referrals, or may offer primary care but not mental health support, so read the listing carefully and ask if you are unsure. Second, check insurance and cost. Ask whether they take your insurance, whether they offer a sliding scale or payment plan, and what you might have to pay out of pocket, and check with your insurance plan, too, because coverage for gender-affirming care varies. Third, check practical details: where the clinic is, how to get there, whether they offer telehealth visits, what their hours are, and how long the wait for a new-patient appointment might be. Telehealth can be a good option if you live far from an affirming provider, or if travelling is hard, but ask whether the provider can offer telehealth in your state, and whether some visits still need to happen in person.

Fourth, look at how the clinic presents itself. Does its website use inclusive language and pictures? Does it mention LGBTQ+ care, pronouns, or a nondiscrimination policy that explicitly protects LGBTQ+ patients? Are there gender-neutral restrooms, or a note about how to ask for a chaperone during an exam? These small details do not prove a provider is affirming, but they are good signs that the clinic has thought about making trans people feel welcome. Read recent reviews, too, especially from LGBTQ+ patients, but take them with a grain of salt, because one person's good experience does not guarantee yours, and one bad review does not always mean the provider is a bad fit. Look for patterns: repeated mentions of misgendering, dismissive care, or long waits are worth paying attention to, and so are repeated mentions of kind, respectful, knowledgeable care.

Questions to ask the front desk

Calling the front desk before you book is one of the best ways to learn how a clinic really works, and you do not need to share your whole story to ask a few simple questions. You can say something like: "Hi, I am looking to establish care and I have a few questions before booking." Then ask the questions that matter most to you. The way the front desk answers, and how they treat you on that first call, will tell you a lot about what a visit might feel like. If they are warm, clear, and respectful, that is a good sign. If they sound confused, dismissive, or unwilling to answer, trust that feeling and consider another provider.

  • Does this provider have experience caring for trans and LGBTQ+ patients?
  • Can my chosen name and pronouns be recorded, and will the front desk, nurses, and clinician all see and use them?
  • Do you take my insurance, or do you offer a sliding scale or other help with cost?
  • Do you offer telehealth visits, and are new patients able to use them?
  • Do you have a nondiscrimination policy that explicitly protects LGBTQ+ patients?
  • Can I bring a support person to my appointment, and can I ask for a chaperone during an exam?
  • Will the provider explain why an exam or test is needed, and can I pause or stop an exam if I need to?
  • How do I get my records, referrals, or prescriptions if I need them?

Write down the answers, and the name of the person you spoke to, so you can compare providers and remember what was promised. If the front desk cannot answer a question, ask if a nurse, a care coordinator, or the provider can call you back. A clinic that is willing to take that extra step is showing you that they care about getting it right.

Red flags to watch for

Trust your gut. If something feels wrong, it probably is, and you do not owe a provider continued access to your care simply because you booked an appointment. Be cautious if a provider or clinic repeatedly uses the wrong name or pronouns after you have corrected them, makes assumptions about your gender, your relationships, or your sex life, or asks intrusive questions that have nothing to do with your symptoms. Be cautious if they call your sexual orientation or gender identity a disorder, say it was caused by trauma or parenting, or try to change, reduce, or fix who you are. Be cautious if they present an exam or a test as automatic, without explaining why it is needed or whether it is right for you, or if they pressure you when you ask questions, rush you, or refuse to explain what they are doing.

Other red flags include a clinic that says "we treat everyone the same" but cannot explain how care is tailored to your needs, a provider who says "I have never dealt with this before" and shows no willingness to learn or refer you to someone who can help, and staff who stare, laugh, make inappropriate comments, or become dismissive when you share who you are. An intake form with no questions about sexual orientation or gender identity can also be a sign that a clinic has not thought about LGBTQ+ patients, although it is not proof on its own. You are allowed to leave an appointment, to ask for a different provider, or to seek care somewhere else, and you are allowed to do that without explaining yourself or apologising. Your health and your dignity matter more than politeness.

Green flags: what good care looks like

It helps to know what to look for, not only what to avoid. Good signs include pronouns listed in a provider's online bio, intake forms that ask about sexual orientation and gender identity, and a website that explicitly mentions LGBTQ+ care and experience. Good signs include staff who use your name and pronouns without being reminded, who ask how you would like to be addressed, and who apologise and correct themselves if they slip up. Good signs include a provider who explains why an exam is needed, what will happen, what your options are, and who asks for your consent before proceeding, and who checks in with you during the exam. Good signs include a provider who asks about your actual health practices and risks, rather than making assumptions, and who listens to your goals and works with you to build a plan that fits your life. And good signs include a clinic that makes it easy to bring a support person, to ask for a chaperone, to get your records, and to ask questions after your visit.

None of these signs alone proves a provider is perfect, but together they paint a picture of a clinic that has done the work to welcome trans people. If you find a provider like that, hold on to them, and tell your friends. If you do not find one right away, keep looking. It can take a few tries to find the right fit, and that is not your fault. You are not being picky, and you are not asking for too much. You are asking for basic, respectful care, and you deserve it.

Bringing a support person, and preparing for your visit

You can bring a support person to your appointment, and for many people, that makes a visit much easier. A friend, partner, family member, or community member can help you remember questions, take notes, advocate for you if you feel overwhelmed, and sit with you in the waiting room. Ask the clinic ahead of time whether a support person can come into the exam room with you, and whether you can ask for a chaperone during an intimate exam. You can also ask whether the clinic has a patient advocate or a care coordinator who can help you navigate the visit. Bring a short list of your questions, your medicines, your allergies, and any records you want the provider to see, and bring your ID and insurance card. If you use a different name from the one on your ID, tell the front desk which name you use and which name is on your records, so they can help you avoid confusion.

After your visit, take a moment to check in with yourself. Did the provider listen to you? Did they use your name and pronouns? Did they explain things clearly? Did you feel safe? If the answer is yes, book your next visit and keep their details somewhere safe. If the answer is no, you do not have to go back, and you can try the next name on your short list. Write down what happened, so you can remember it later, and consider sharing your experience with a community group or a directory, so other people can learn from it. Finding affirming care is a process, and every step you take, even a visit that does not work out, brings you closer to a provider who will treat you well.
