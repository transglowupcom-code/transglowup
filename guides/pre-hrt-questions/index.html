Questions to Ask Before Starting HRT

Starting hormone therapy is a big, hopeful step, and it is also a medical decision. Going in with clear questions helps you understand what may change, what will take time, and what support you will need along the way.

November 6, 2026 · 6 min read

Start with your goals, not a timeline you saw online

Every body responds to hormone therapy in its own way, and your age, genetics, health history, and the form of hormones you use can all shape what you notice and when you notice it. That is why comparing your first months to someone else's photos or posts can leave you feeling behind when you are not behind at all. A good first conversation is about your goals: what changes matter most to you, what changes you would rather avoid if you can, and what a good result would look like for you in a year or two, not just in a few weeks. Your provider can then talk through which changes tend to come earlier, which tend to take longer, and which changes are often permanent even if you later stop hormones. For example, Planned Parenthood notes that estrogen-based therapy may bring breast growth, body fat moving toward the hips, thighs, face, and chest, softer skin, less muscle mass, and changes in mood and libido, while testosterone-based therapy may bring a deeper voice, more facial and body hair, increased muscle mass, and periods stopping, usually within a few months. Those lists are starting points for a conversation, not promises about your body. Ask your provider to walk through them slowly, to say which effects they expect to be gradual, and to be honest about what is uncertain. You deserve a plan that fits your goals, not a generic plan you have to squeeze yourself into.

It also helps to ask what hormone therapy will not do. Estrogen, for instance, does not usually change the pitch of a voice that deepened during a testosterone-dominant puberty, because testosterone can thicken the vocal cords in a way medication does not reverse. That does not mean your voice cannot change — voice training can help where hormones do not — but it is kinder to learn that before you start than to wait months for a change that was never likely. Ask the same kind of question about hair, height, bone structure, and anything else you are hoping for. A provider who answers plainly, even when the answer is disappointing, is giving you something valuable: a clear picture you can plan around.

Ask about effects in general terms — and what is permanent

Hormone therapy works slowly, more like a second puberty than a switch you flip. Fenway Health's consent information explains that masculinizing changes from testosterone may take several months to become noticeable and usually take three to five years to be complete, and feminizing changes are also generally considered complete after two to three years. That long horizon can feel frustrating when you are eager to see yourself more clearly, and it can also be reassuring: you do not have to decide everything on day one, and your provider can adjust your care as your body responds and your goals become clearer. Ask how often you will check in during the first year, how changes to your plan are usually decided, and what signs should prompt you to call sooner rather than waiting for the next visit. Ask what to expect if you miss a dose, change how you take your hormones, or need to pause for surgery or another health reason, so a small disruption does not turn into a panic.

Some changes tend to stay even if hormones stop, and others tend to fade. Your provider should be able to explain that difference for the plan you are considering, in plain language, without rushing you. For testosterone, voice deepening is one of the changes that is generally permanent, while for estrogen, breast growth generally remains. Fat redistribution, on the other hand, tends to shift back over time if hormones stop. Those are general patterns, not guarantees, and your provider can explain what they mean for you. If a provider will not talk about permanence, or treats every question as a sign you are not ready, that is useful information too. Readiness is not about having no doubts. It is about understanding the effects, the risks, and the unknowns well enough to make an informed choice — and informed consent care is built around exactly that idea.

Think about fertility before you start

This is the question that is hardest to revisit later, so please give it real time before your first dose. Hormone therapy can reduce fertility, and UCSF Transgender Care advises people considering estrogen therapy to assume that within a few months of starting, they could permanently lose the ability to create sperm. Some people do keep some sperm production, or see it return after stopping hormones, but UCSF is clear that you should not count on that. For testosterone, fertility may also be reduced, periods often stop, and it may become difficult for the ovaries to release eggs, especially after longer use. Some people do become pregnant after stopping testosterone, sometimes with help from a fertility specialist, but that is not guaranteed either. The safest way to think about it is this: if having a biological child might matter to you one day, even if you are not sure now, talk about preserving that option before you start hormones.

Preservation usually means freezing sperm or freezing eggs before hormones begin, and your provider can explain what that involves, how long it typically takes, and where to get a referral. It is also important to know that hormone therapy is not reliable birth control. Planned Parenthood and UCSF both note that pregnancy is still possible while taking hormones, even when periods have stopped, so if pregnancy would be unwanted, ask about contraception that fits your body and your plan. If you are already on hormones and this question is new to you, do not blame yourself for not asking sooner. Bring it up at your next visit anyway. A good provider will talk through your options now, without judgment, and help you decide what, if anything, you still want to explore.

Tip: Write down your honest answer to this question before your visit: “If I could never have a biological child, would I be at peace with that in five years? In ten?” Bring that note with you. It makes a hard conversation much easier to start.

What bloodwork and monitoring usually involve

Bloodwork is how your provider checks that hormones are working safely for your body, and it is a normal part of care, not a test you can pass or fail. At a first visit, your provider will usually review your medical history, check your blood pressure and other vital signs, and order lab tests to look for possible side effects and to set a baseline before you start. Planned Parenthood explains that lab work is usually done more often in the first year — often around every three months — and less often once you are on a steady plan, often about once a year after that. Your exact schedule may be different if you have other health conditions, change your plan, or have results that need a closer look, and your provider should explain why they are choosing the schedule they suggest for you.

What is checked depends on the hormones you take. For people taking estrogen, providers commonly follow estrogen levels. For people taking testosterone, providers commonly follow testosterone levels and red blood cell counts, because testosterone can raise those counts. If you take a testosterone blocker such as spironolactone, your provider may also check your kidney function and potassium levels. Other labs, such as a cholesterol check, a diabetes screening, or liver function tests, may be recommended depending on your health history. You do not need to memorize that list. What matters is asking these questions: which labs will you order for me, what are you watching for, how will I get my results, and who do I contact if a result worries me? Ask how to prepare, too — for example, whether the timing of your lab draw should relate to when you last took your hormones — because your provider's instructions may depend on how you take them. And if needles, labs, or medical settings are hard for you, say so. A good clinic can talk through options, including having labs drawn at a location that feels easier for you.

Mental-health check-ins are part of the care, not a hurdle

Many people feel a lift in mood once their body starts to match who they are, and many people also feel tender, tired, or emotionally raw at times while hormones settle in. Both can be true, and neither means you made a wrong choice. Ask your provider how they like to check in about mood, sleep, anxiety, and low mood, and what support they can offer if things feel heavy. Some clinics use an informed consent model, which means you do not need a letter from a therapist or a referral to receive hormone therapy — Planned Parenthood is clear about this at many of its health centers, and your provider should be able to tell you whether that is how their clinic works. Even when a letter is not required, mental-health support can still be a wonderful part of your care, especially if you are navigating family stress, work stress, dysphoria, or the grief that can come with waiting so long to be seen as yourself.

It helps to ask directly: do you have therapists or support groups you trust for trans and nonbinary patients, and can you refer me if I want that support? What should I do if my mood drops sharply, if I feel unsafe, or if I notice thoughts of harming myself? You deserve a clear answer to that question before you leave, not a vague reassurance. If you already have a therapist, ask whether your medical provider and your therapist can work as a team, with your permission, so you are not carrying the same story between two offices. And remember that needing support is not a sign that you are not ready for hormones. It is a sign that you are a person going through a big life change, and that you deserve care for your whole self, not just your hormone levels.

How to choose a provider you can trust

The right provider is not just someone who can prescribe hormones. It is someone who listens, explains things in plain language, uses your name and pronouns, and treats you as the expert on your own goals. Before you book, it is fair to ask how much experience the clinic has with trans and nonbinary patients, whether they follow guidance such as the WPATH Standards of Care and UCSF's guidelines, and whether they use an informed consent model. You can also ask practical questions: how long is a first visit, what happens at that visit, will you need an exam, and can you bring someone with you for support? Planned Parenthood notes that a typical first visit may take one to two hours, that you will usually not be asked to change into a gown or have an exam unless you request one for a particular concern, and that follow-up visits are usually shorter. Those details vary by clinic, so ask your clinic what to expect — and notice how they answer. A clinic that welcomes questions before you book is already showing you how it will treat you in the room.

Cost and coverage matter too, and it is not rude to ask about them. Ask whether the clinic takes your insurance, what a visit and labs may cost if you are paying out of pocket, whether there is a sliding scale or payment help, and whether refills depend on keeping up with visits and labs. Ask how to reach the clinic between visits, how prescription refills work, and how much notice to give when you need one. If a clinic is far away, ask whether any follow-ups can be done by phone or video, and where you would go for labs nearby. If the first provider you meet is not a good fit — if they rush you, dismiss your questions, misgender you repeatedly, or push you toward a plan you did not choose — you are allowed to look for another provider. That is not being difficult. That is taking care of yourself.

Bring a list to your appointment

Appointments can go fast, and it is easy to forget the question that mattered most once you are sitting in the exam room. A short written list fixes that. Bring your goals in your own words, your medical history and current medicines, any allergies, and the questions below — or your own version of them. You can hand the list to your provider at the start and say, “These are the things I want to make sure we cover today.” Most good providers love that. It helps them help you, and it means you leave with answers instead of a nagging feeling that you forgot something.

  • What changes can I expect from this plan, in general terms, and which ones tend to be permanent?
  • What are the risks and side effects I should know about, and what signs should I call you about?
  • What should I think about for fertility before I start, and can you refer me for preservation if I want it?
  • What bloodwork will you order, how often, and how will I get my results?
  • How do you check in about mood and mental health, and what support can you offer or refer me to?
  • Do you use informed consent, and do I need a letter from a therapist to start?
  • How will we decide about changes to my plan, and what happens if I miss a dose or need to pause?
  • What will visits and labs cost, do you take my insurance, and how do refills work?
  • Can I bring someone with me, and how do I reach you between visits?
  • Is there anything in my health history that changes what plan is safest for me?

After your visit, take a few minutes to write down what was decided, what your next steps are, and when your next visit and labs are due. If something was unclear, call or message the clinic and ask. You do not have to understand everything the first time. You just have to keep asking until it makes sense — and you are allowed to take your time, to get a second opinion, and to start when you feel ready. You have waited long enough to be yourself. You deserve care that honors that, at a pace you can trust.
