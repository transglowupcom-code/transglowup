Recovery First Steps After Gender-Affirming Surgery

Surgery is a big step, and recovery takes time, help, and patience. This guide covers the general first steps — and your surgeon's instructions always come first.

November 6, 2026 · 6 min read

First, the most important rule

This article is general information, not medical advice, and it cannot replace the instructions from your own surgeon and surgical team. Every surgery is different, every body heals differently, and your surgeon knows your procedure, your health history, and what your body needs. If anything in this guide seems to disagree with what your surgeon told you, follow your surgeon's instructions. Keep their written aftercare instructions somewhere easy to reach, keep their phone number and after-hours number saved in your phone, and write down the name of the person to call if you are worried. If you lose the instructions or you are unsure what they mean, call the surgical team and ask. Asking a question is never a bother, and it is always better than guessing.

Recovery times vary a lot depending on the type of surgery you had. Some chest surgeries need at least a week of recovery at home and several weeks of avoiding strenuous activity, while some genital surgeries need months of follow-up care and a longer healing period. That range is normal, and it is not a race. Your surgeon will tell you what to expect after your particular procedure, including how long to rest, when to come back for check-ups, and when you can slowly return to work, exercise, and other activities. Try to plan for the longer end of what they describe, so you are not caught out if healing takes a little more time than you hoped. Giving yourself that breathing room is a kindness to your future self.

Before surgery: set up your recovery space

A little preparation before surgery makes the first days at home much easier, because you will be sore, tired, and less able to reach, lift, or move around than usual. Arrange for someone to drive you home after surgery and stay with you at least the first night, and ideally for the first few days. You will need help with everyday things like cooking, laundry, shopping, cleaning, and caring for pets or other people in your home, so plan for that support for about a month to six weeks after surgery, or for as long as your surgeon advises. If you live alone, ask a friend, family member, or community member if they can stay over or check in daily, and be specific about what would help most.

Set up a clean, comfortable recovery space before you leave for surgery. Choose a bed or couch you can get in and out of without using your arms much, and keep pillows nearby so you can prop yourself up comfortably. A recliner or wedge pillows can help for the first few days or weeks, especially if you need to sleep on your back. Stock the space with the basics: food and water within easy reach, any medicines your surgeon prescribed, clean towels and bedding, phone chargers with a long cord, a bendy straw so you can drink without sitting up fully, and something gentle to do while you rest, like a book, a show, or music. Keep the space clean, because clean surroundings help with wound care, and keep your surgeon's instructions, your appointment list, and a notebook for questions all in one place. If you can, practise sleeping on your back before surgery, so the change feels less strange afterwards, and fill any prescriptions ahead of time so you can go straight home and rest.

Rest, pain, and taking your medicines as directed

Some soreness, swelling, and bruising after surgery are expected, and pain is usually worst in the first few days and then eases gradually. Your surgeon will prescribe medicines to help manage pain, and you should take them exactly as directed, including taking them with food if the instructions say so. Do not skip doses to tough it out, and do not take extra doses or add other pain medicines, vitamins, or supplements without checking with your surgical team first. Keep a simple note of when you took each dose, so you do not lose track when you are tired. If pain is getting worse instead of better, or if the medicine does not seem to help at all, call your surgeon and tell them, rather than waiting for your next appointment.

Rest is not laziness during recovery; it is the work your body is doing to heal. Sleep when you are tired, keep your days quiet in the first week, and let other people handle the chores. At the same time, your surgeon may ask you to get up and move a little each day, and that gentle movement matters, too. Short, slow walks around your home, as your surgeon allows, can help your circulation and your mood, and can help prevent stiffness. The key word is gentle. Avoid lifting, pushing, or pulling anything heavy, avoid strenuous activity, and avoid stretching or reaching overhead until your surgeon says it is safe. Your surgeon will give you detailed instructions for when you can return to work, drive, exercise, and lift things again, and those instructions depend on your surgery, so do not borrow a timeline from someone else's recovery.

Wound care, the basics — as your surgeon directs

Good wound care helps prevent infection and helps your body heal well, but the exact steps depend on your surgery, so this section is only a general outline. Your surgical team will tell you how to clean your wound, what products to use, how often to change dressings, and when you can shower or bathe. Follow those steps exactly, and do not add creams, ointments, or home remedies unless your surgeon has told you to. In general, keep the surgical area clean and dry, wash your hands carefully before you touch dressings or the wound, change dressings as directed, and avoid soaking the area in a bath or swimming until your surgeon says it is safe. Wear loose, soft clothing that does not rub or press on the wound, and avoid tight clothing that could irritate the area or cause friction.

Some surgeries involve drains, tubes that drain blood and fluid from the surgical area for the first week or two, and some involve dressings or bandages that stay in place until your first follow-up visit. If you have drains, your team will show you how to care for them and what to watch for, and they will usually remove them at a follow-up visit. A small amount of fluid or crusting around a wound in the early days can be normal, but you should still tell your surgeon about anything that worries you, because they would rather hear from you early than late. Keep track of what the wound looks like each day, in a few plain words, so you can describe any changes clearly if you call. Take photos only if your surgeon asks you to, and keep them private.

Do not smoke, vape, or use nicotine products during recovery unless your surgeon has specifically discussed it with you, because nicotine can affect healing and increase the risk of complications. Surgeons often ask people to stop using nicotine for weeks or months before and after surgery for that reason. The same care applies to alcohol and other substances: ask your surgical team what is safe, and be honest with them about what you use, so they can give you advice that fits your real situation. Eating regular, nourishing meals and drinking enough water also supports healing, even when your appetite is low, so keep simple, easy foods nearby and eat little and often if big meals feel like too much.

Warning signs: call your surgeon promptly

Call your surgeon or surgical team promptly if you notice bleeding, severe pain, pain that is getting worse instead of better, increasing redness or swelling around the wound, discharge from the wound, a wound that opens up, a fever, or any unusual symptom that worries you. Do not wait for your next appointment to mention these things. If you have chest pain, trouble breathing, or any symptom that feels urgent or frightening, seek urgent medical care right away. Keep your surgeon's number where you and your support person can both find it, and write down what you noticed and when it started, so you can describe it clearly on the phone. You are not overreacting by calling, and early calls can prevent bigger problems.

  • Bleeding from the wound, or swelling that grows quickly
  • Severe pain, or pain that keeps getting worse
  • Increasing redness, swelling, warmth, or discharge around the wound
  • A wound that opens, or skin that looks very different from the day before
  • A fever, chills, or feeling much more unwell than expected
  • Chest pain, trouble breathing, or any urgent, frightening symptom — seek urgent care right away

Follow-up visits matter

Go to every follow-up appointment your surgeon schedules, even if you feel fine. Those visits are how your surgeon checks that you are healing well, removes dressings, drains, or stitches when the time is right, and answers questions about what comes next. Bring your notebook with you, and write down what they tell you about wound care, activity, and the next stage of recovery. If you miss an appointment or need to reschedule, call the clinic as soon as you can to book another time. If you are travelling for surgery, ask your team how long you should stay near the hospital after discharge, and plan for at least one follow-up visit before you travel home. Keep in touch with your primary care clinician, too, because surgery is one part of your transition and your ongoing care, and they can help you with other health needs while you recover.

The emotional side of recovery

Recovery is not only physical, and the feelings that come with it can surprise you. Many people feel relief, joy, and excitement after surgery, and also feel tired, frustrated, anxious, or low at different points along the way. That mix is common. Your body is healing, your sleep may be broken, you may be in pain, you may not be able to do the things you usually do, and the results may take months to settle into their final shape, with swelling and bruising fading slowly. It is normal for healing to feel uneven: a good day can be followed by a sore day, and that does not mean something has gone wrong. Give yourself permission to feel whatever you feel, without deciding that one feeling means the surgery was a mistake or that another feeling means you have to be happy all the time.

Stay connected to people who care about you, even in small ways. A short message, a visit from a friend, a call with someone who has been through surgery, or a support group can make a long recovery feel less lonely. Ongoing care with a mental health provider can also help with the stress of surgery and recovery, and your surgical team can refer you if you would like that support. If you feel very low, very anxious, or unable to cope, reach out to your clinician, a trusted person, or a crisis support service in your area. You do not have to carry those feelings alone, and asking for help is part of recovering well. Be patient with your body, celebrate small steps, like a comfortable walk or a good night's sleep, and remember that healing takes the time it takes.

A gentle first-week checklist

Keep your surgeon's instructions and phone number close. Take medicines as directed and note each dose. Rest a lot, move gently as your surgeon allows, and avoid lifting and strenuous activity. Care for your wound exactly as your team showed you, keep it clean and dry, and go to every follow-up visit. Ask for help with meals, chores, and rides, and tell your surgeon promptly about any warning sign. Be kind to yourself on the hard days.
