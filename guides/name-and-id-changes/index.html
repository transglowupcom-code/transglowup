Changing Your Name and ID Documents

Getting your name right on your ID is a big, affirming step — and it takes a few steps in the right order. This guide walks you through the usual path, one piece at a time.

November 6, 2026 · 6 min read

First, a gentle note

This guide is general information, not legal advice, and it cannot replace the rules for your own state or the instructions from the agencies you will deal with. The process for changing a name and updating ID documents varies by state, and it changes over time, so always check the current guidance for your state before you start, and check the official pages for Social Security and for passports before you file anything. The best starting point for state-by-state rules is the ID Documents Center from Advocates for Trans Equality, which has a page for each state explaining what that state requires. If you can, ask a local LGBTQ+ legal aid organisation, a trans community group, or a lawyer for help with your state's forms, especially if your situation is complicated. You do not have to do this alone, and getting help early can save you a second trip or a rejected application later.

It also helps to know that this process takes patience. You will be dealing with several different offices, each with its own forms, documents, and way of doing things, and you may need to wait between steps while one record updates before the next office can check it. That waiting is frustrating, but it is normal, and it does not mean you have done anything wrong. Take it one step at a time, keep copies of everything, and celebrate each piece as it lands in your new name. Your name is yours, and every document you update is a step toward a life where your ID matches who you are.

The usual order, in brief

For most people, the usual order is: first, get a court order legally changing your name; then update your name with Social Security; then update your driver's license or state ID; then update your passport; and then work through the rest of your records, like your birth certificate, bank, employer, school, and insurance. The reason for this order is that the offices check each other. Social Security is the record that other agencies check against, so updating it early helps the later steps go smoothly. Your driver's license office will usually check your name against the Social Security record, and your passport application will need proof of your legal name change, so having the earlier steps done first prevents mismatches and extra trips.

There are exceptions, and your state may do things a little differently, so treat this order as the usual path, not a rule that fits everyone. If you changed your name through marriage or divorce, for example, your marriage certificate or divorce decree may be the document that proves your name change, rather than a separate court order. If you do not have a passport yet, that step may not apply to you right now. If you were born in a different state from the one where you live now, your birth certificate will follow the rules of the state that issued it, which may be different from the rules where you live. Read your state's page in the ID Documents Center first, make a short list of the steps that apply to you, and work through them in order.

Step 1: The court name change

In most states, legally changing your name starts with a petition to a court in the county or parish where you live. You will usually need to fill in a form asking the court to change your name, give your current legal name and the new name you want, and explain briefly why you are asking. Some states ask for background information, and some have rules about how long you must have lived in the state before you can apply. The court may schedule a hearing, where a judge reviews your petition and may ask you a few questions, or in some states a hearing is not required. Some states used to require you to publish your name change in a newspaper, and some still do, while others no longer require it, so check what your state asks for now.

If the judge approves your petition, you will receive a court order authorising your name change. That court order is the key document for everything that follows, so treat it with care. Ask the court clerk for several certified copies when you receive it, because many offices will want to see a certified copy, not a photocopy you made at home. A certified copy is a copy that the court has stamped or signed to confirm it is a true copy of the original. Keep the original in a safe place at home, and keep the certified copies in a folder you can take with you to appointments. If you lose a certified copy, you can usually order another one from the court that issued it, but it is easier to have spares from the start. Write down the exact spelling of your new name, including any hyphens, capital letters, or middle names, and use that exact spelling on every form from now on, so your records match each other.

Choosing your new name is a personal decision, and it is worth taking a moment to get it right before you file. Think about the full name you want on your ID, including your first, middle, and last names, and how you want it spelled. Check that the spelling is the one you want to live with, because changing it again later means going through the process again. If you are under eighteen, the rules are different: a parent or guardian usually has to file on your behalf, and both parents may need to be notified, depending on your state. If you have questions about your situation, especially if you have a criminal record, are on probation or parole, or are in a complicated family situation, a local legal aid organisation can help you understand what your state allows.

Step 2: Social Security

Once you have your court order, or your marriage certificate or divorce decree if that is your name-change document, the next step is usually to update your name with the Social Security Administration. Social Security does not update your name automatically when a court changes it; you have to ask them to update it, and you have to show them proof. You will need to fill in their application for a Social Security card, and bring or send proof of your legal name change, proof of your identity, and proof of your citizenship or lawful immigration status. The proof of your name change must be the original document or a certified copy from the court or agency that issued it. A plain photocopy is not enough, and a notarised copy is not the same as a certified copy.

You can usually apply in person at a Social Security office, or by mail, depending on what your local office offers. Find your nearest office through the Social Security Administration's website, and check whether you need an appointment. Bring your documents with you, and ask the office what they need from you before you go, so you do not have to make a second trip. Your Social Security number stays the same; only the name on the record changes. You do not need to wait for the new card to arrive in the mail before you start using your new name with employers, banks, or other organisations, because you can show them your court order as proof while the card is on its way. Give the Social Security record a little time to update in their system before you go to the next step, because the driver's license office checks against that record, and going too soon can mean being turned away and having to come back.

Step 3: Your driver's license or state ID

After Social Security has your new name, the next step is usually your driver's license or state ID. Every state handles this a little differently, but the general pattern is the same: you visit your state's licensing agency in person, which may be called the DMV, the BMV, the MVC, the RMV, or the Secretary of State, depending on where you live, and you bring a certified copy of your court order, your current license or ID, and any other documents your state asks for, such as proof of where you live. The office will check your name against the Social Security record, so make sure that step is done first. Most states ask you to visit in person for a name change, and there may be a charge for a replacement card, which varies by state, so check your state's current guidance before you go.

While you are at the licensing office, it is a good time to ask about updating the gender marker on your license or ID, if that is something you want to do, because the rules for that also vary by state. Some states let you choose a marker that matches your identity, and some offer an option beyond the usual two markers, while others have stricter rules or require extra documents. Check your state's page in the ID Documents Center for the current rules, and ask the office what they need from you. If you are also due to renew your license, or if you want a REAL ID, ask whether you can do those things in the same visit, so you save yourself another trip. When you leave, check the new license carefully to make sure your name is spelled exactly as you want it, and keep your old license until the new one arrives, if the office tells you to.

Step 4: Your passport

Your passport is the next step after your license, and the process depends on your situation. The U.S. Department of State explains that the way to change your name on a passport varies depending on when your passport was issued and how your name changed. If your name changed recently and your passport was issued recently, you may be able to use a correction process by mail, with your current passport, a passport photo, and the original or certified document that shows your name changed. If more time has passed, you may need to renew by mail or apply in person, with your current passport, a photo, and your name-change document. The exact form you need depends on your situation, so read the State Department's name-change page carefully and follow the instructions for the box that matches you, rather than guessing which form to use.

If you do not have a passport yet, you will apply for your first passport in your new name, with your court order and your other identity documents. If you have travel coming up, plan ahead and do not send your passport away close to a trip, because you will not be able to travel internationally while it is being processed. Keep a copy of your passport and your court order in a safe place separate from the originals, and check your new passport carefully when it arrives to make sure your name is spelled correctly. If there is a mistake, the State Department has a process for correcting printing or data errors, and you should follow that process promptly. As with your license, you can also check the current rules for updating the gender marker on a passport, because those rules have changed over time and may be different from what you have heard before.

Step 5: Birth certificate and everything else

After the main government IDs are updated, you will need to work through the rest of your records. Your birth certificate is updated through the vital records office in the state that issued it, not necessarily the state where you live now, and that state will usually ask for a certified copy of your court order. The rules for birth certificates vary a lot by state, so check the ID Documents Center page for the state that issued your birth certificate, and follow that state's instructions. Some people update their birth certificate right away, and others leave it for later, depending on what they need it for, and both choices are fine.

Then comes the long list of everyday records: your bank and credit cards, your employer and payroll, your school or college, your health insurance, your doctor and pharmacy, your car registration and insurance, your voter registration, your utilities, your phone, and any subscriptions or accounts in your old name. Work through them a few at a time, starting with the ones that matter most to you, like your bank and your employer, because those are the places where a name mismatch can cause the most trouble. For each one, ask what proof they need: most will accept a certified copy of your court order, or your new license or Social Security card, but some may ask for the original, so bring your folder with you and ask before you hand anything over. Keep a list of who you have told and who is still to do, and tick them off as you go, so you can see your progress.

Keeping copies of everything

Keep copies of everything, from the start. Make a folder, physical or digital, with a copy of your court order, your old and new IDs, your Social Security card, your passport, and every letter or receipt you receive along the way. Keep the originals and certified copies in a safe place at home, and keep photocopies or scans in a separate place, so you have a backup if something is lost. Write down the date you filed each application, the office you dealt with, the name of the person who helped you, and any reference number they gave you. If an office says they need a document you do not have, ask them to write down exactly what they need, so you can bring the right thing next time. Good records save you time, money, and stress, and they help you prove your name change if any office asks about it later.

  • Ask the court for several certified copies of your court order
  • Keep originals safe at home, and carry certified copies to appointments
  • Make photocopies or scans as backups, stored separately from the originals
  • Note the date, office, person, and any reference number for each step
  • Use the exact same spelling of your new name on every form

If you get stuck

If an office turns you away, asks for a document you do not have, or tells you something that does not match what you read online, stay calm and ask questions. Ask them to explain what they need, in writing if possible, and ask if there is a supervisor or a different office that can help. Rules can be confusing, and staff do not always know the latest guidance, especially for trans name changes, so it is okay to double-check with a legal aid organisation or with the ID Documents Center before you give up. If you cannot afford a lawyer, many LGBTQ+ organisations offer free help with name changes, and some run clinics where volunteers help you fill in forms. You deserve to have your name right, and you deserve help getting there.
