What to Ask at Your First Affirming-Care Visit

Your first visit with an affirming provider should feel like being welcomed, not tested. A little preparation — a list, your name and pronouns, and questions you actually care about — helps you get the care you deserve.

November 6, 2026 · 6 min read

Before you book: you are allowed to vet your provider

Finding affirming care is not just about finding a clinic that lists gender-affirming services. It is about finding people who will treat you with respect, listen to your goals, and explain your options in plain language. You are allowed to ask questions before you book, and a good clinic will welcome that. Ask how much experience the provider has working with trans and nonbinary patients, what training they have had in affirming care, and whether they are familiar with guidance such as the WPATH Standards of Care, the main international clinical guideline for transgender health. Ask whether they use an informed consent model for hormone therapy, which means they will explain the risks, benefits, and alternatives and trust you to decide, without requiring a letter from a therapist. Planned Parenthood is clear that at many of its health centers, you do not need to participate in therapy or get a referral to receive hormone therapy, and you do not need a note from a mental health therapist. Not every clinic works that way, so ask your clinic directly — and notice whether they answer plainly or make you feel like you are asking too much. You are not.

It also helps to ask practical questions before you go: how long is a first visit, what will happen there, will there be an exam, and can you bring someone with you? Planned Parenthood notes that a typical first visit for gender-affirming care may take one to two hours, that follow-up visits are usually shorter, and that you will usually not be asked to change into a gown or have an exam unless you request one for a particular concern. Other testing, such as pregnancy, sexually transmitted infection, or cervical cancer screening, may be recommended but is not required. Your clinic may be different, so ask what to expect at your clinic, and ask how to reach them between visits. If you are nervous about calling, you can write your questions down and read them out loud, or ask a friend to call with you. You deserve a clinic that makes that first step feel doable.

Preparing your list

Appointments can go fast, and it is easy to forget the question that mattered most once you are in the room. A short written list fixes that, and most good providers love it when you bring one. Start with your goals in your own words: what brings you in today, what changes you are hoping for, and what you want to understand better before you decide anything. Then add your medical history — current medicines, allergies, past surgeries, and any health conditions — and any questions about your body, your mood, or your daily life that feel relevant. You do not have to share more than you are comfortable sharing, and you can ask why a question is being asked if it feels intrusive. A provider who explains why they need information is showing you respect. A provider who demands information without explaining why is showing you something else.

Bring a pen, or use your phone, so you can write down answers as you go. If you take notes, you will leave with a record you can look back on, instead of trying to remember everything later. It also helps to bring a list of the medicines you take, including any hormones, supplements, or over-the-counter medicines, and to know whether you have insurance and what it covers, if you can. If you do not know, that is okay — you can ask the clinic to help you figure it out. And if you use a different name than your legal name, or different pronouns than the ones on your ID, write those down too, so you can hand them to the front desk and to your provider without having to explain them out loud in a busy waiting room if you do not want to.

  • What brings you in today, in your own words, and what are your goals for the next year?
  • What medicines do you take now, and do you have any allergies or health conditions?
  • What questions do you have about hormone therapy, fertility, or other gender-affirming care?
  • What mental-health support, referrals, or community resources do you want to know about?
  • What are your insurance, cost, and follow-up questions?
  • Who can you contact between visits, and how do refills and lab results work?

Name, pronouns, and forms

Your name and pronouns are not a detail. They are how you deserve to be addressed, in the waiting room, in the exam room, and in your records. Many affirming clinics ask for your name and pronouns separately from your legal information on intake forms, and that is a good sign. When you book, you can say, “My name is ___, my pronouns are ___, and my legal name on my insurance is ___ if that is different.” You can ask the clinic to put your name and pronouns where the front desk, the nurse, and the provider will all see them, and to use them when they call you from the waiting room. If a form only offers “male” or “female,” or does not have a place for your name and pronouns, that is useful information about the clinic — and you can still write your name and pronouns in the margin, or tell the front desk directly.

Mistakes happen, even in good clinics, and how a provider handles a mistake tells you a lot. A good provider apologizes briefly, corrects themselves, and moves on, without making you comfort them or explain your identity. A provider who keeps misgendering you after you correct them, who debates whether your identity is real or a phase, or who asks you to educate them about basic terms, is not giving you affirming care. You do not have to stay and teach them. You can correct them once, clearly, and see what happens next. If the pattern continues, you are allowed to end the visit, to ask for a different provider, or to look for another clinic. You are also allowed to ask how the clinic handles names and pronouns on insurance claims and in referrals, because those systems sometimes use a legal name even when the clinic uses your chosen name in person. Asking that question helps you avoid surprises later, and it helps the clinic get it right.

Questions about HRT readiness, referrals, and mental-health support

If hormone therapy is on your mind, your first visit is a good time to ask about it — even if you are not sure you want to start yet. Ask what the clinic's process looks like: what a first hormone therapy visit involves, whether they use informed consent, whether a therapist's letter is required at their clinic, and how long it usually takes from a first visit to a prescription if you decide to start. Planned Parenthood explains that at a first visit, a clinician will review your medical history, discuss your transition goals, explain the risks and benefits, order lab work to watch for possible side effects, and answer your questions, and that many patients are able to receive a prescription that day if hormone therapy is a good fit. Your clinic may have a different timeline, and it is fair to ask what theirs is. Also ask about fertility before you start, about what bloodwork and follow-up visits usually involve, and about what happens if you need to pause or change your plan.

Ask about referrals, too. Affirming care is rarely just one visit or one prescription. You may want a referral to a therapist who works with trans and nonbinary people, to a voice coach or speech-language pathologist, to a surgeon or a fertility clinic, or to a support group or community resource. A good provider will have names they trust, and will help you navigate those next steps rather than leaving you to figure them out alone. Ask about mental-health support directly: do you have therapists or counselors you recommend, do you offer support groups, and what should I do if I feel low, anxious, or unsafe between visits? You deserve a clear answer to that last question, not a vague reassurance. If you already have a therapist, ask whether your medical provider can work with them, with your permission, so you are not carrying the same story between two offices. And remember that asking for mental-health support is not a sign that you are not ready for other care. It is a sign that you are taking care of your whole self.

Tip: If you are not sure what to ask first, start with this: “What do you need to know about me to help me well, and what can you tell me about how this clinic works?” That one question opens the door to almost everything else.

Insurance, cost, and practical questions

Cost is not a rude question. It is a practical one, and a good clinic will answer it plainly. Before or during your visit, ask whether the clinic takes your insurance, whether the provider you are seeing is in network, and what a visit, labs, and prescriptions may cost if you are paying out of pocket. Ask whether there is a sliding scale, payment help, or a patient navigator who can help you understand your coverage. Ask how prescription refills work, how much notice to give, and whether refills depend on keeping up with visits and labs. Planned Parenthood notes that requesting refills at least one to two weeks in advance, and going to all of your visits and completing your blood tests as scheduled, can help you get refills on time. Your clinic may have its own timing, so ask what theirs is — and ask how you will get lab results, and who to contact if a result worries you.

Also ask about letters and paperwork, if that is relevant to you. Some people need a letter of support for a gender marker change on a birth certificate or other document, or for surgery, and Planned Parenthood notes that its teams can help navigate that process and provide letters of support for government agencies, insurance companies, and other providers. Ask whether your provider writes those letters, whether there is an extra fee or a wait, and what they need from you to do it. If you need time off work, a note for school, or help with a form, ask about that too. A clinic that helps with paperwork is a clinic that understands that affirming care is not just what happens in the exam room — it is also what helps you live your life outside it.

What a good provider does — and what a red flag looks like

Good affirming care has a feeling to it, and you can trust that feeling. A good provider listens more than they talk, asks what your goals are before they tell you what to do, explains why an exam or test is recommended before they do it, and asks for your consent — and keeps asking, so you can pause or stop at any time. They use your name and pronouns, they answer your questions directly, and they do not make you prove your identity or justify why you came. They follow your lead on what to focus on, they apologize briefly if they make a mistake, and they help you get referrals and support without making you feel like a burden. If you bring a support person, a good provider welcomes them, within the clinic's policies, and treats them as part of your care team, not as an interruption.

Red flags are also worth naming plainly, so you can recognize them if they happen. Be cautious if a provider treats your identity as a disorder, tries to change or “fix” your sexual orientation or gender identity, blames all of your problems on being trans, or refuses to use your name or pronouns. Be cautious if they present an exam as automatic without explaining whether it is appropriate for you, if they pressure you when you ask questions, if they make assumptions about your life or your relationships, or if staff stare, laugh, or make inappropriate comments. A guide to finding an affirming therapist lists similar signs to walk away from, including a provider who calls your identity a disorder, tries to change it, or refuses to use your name and pronouns, and notes that major medical associations oppose efforts to change someone's sexual orientation or gender identity. If you feel unsafe, you can say, “I would like to stop here,” and leave. You do not owe a provider continued access to your care simply because you booked an appointment. Making an appointment does not mean consenting to every examination or procedure, and you can always seek care elsewhere.

Bringing someone with you, and what to do after the visit

You do not have to go alone. Many clinics allow you to bring a support person, a friend, a partner, or a family member, and some guides specifically suggest asking about support-person and chaperone policies, especially for intimate examinations. A support person can help you remember questions, take notes, sit with you in the waiting room, or simply be there so you do not have to carry the whole visit by yourself. If you want someone with you in the exam room, ask the clinic when you book, so there are no surprises. If you would rather go alone, that is completely fine too. The point is that you get to choose what helps you feel safest and most comfortable.

After the visit, take a few minutes to write down what was decided while it is still fresh: what your provider recommended, what your next steps are, when your next visit and labs are due, and who to contact if you have questions. If something was unclear, call or message the clinic and ask. You do not have to understand everything the first time, and you do not have to make every decision in one visit. If the visit went well, you can book your follow-up and feel proud of yourself for showing up. If it did not go well, you can write down what happened, talk it through with someone you trust, and look for another provider. Either way, you showed up for yourself, and that matters. You deserve care that sees you clearly, listens to you carefully, and helps you move toward the life you want — one visit, one question, and one kind provider at a time.
