Substance-specific guide

Evidence-based ways forward

There is no single “best” route. Medication can be essential for alcohol, opioid, and nicotine dependence; structured behavioral care is the main evidence-based approach for stimulant, cocaine, and cannabis use disorders.

Alcohol

Talk with a clinician before stopping after regular heavy use. Naltrexone, acamprosate, or disulfiram may help; counseling and mutual support improve the plan.

NIAAA treatment guide

Opioids

Medication is first-line treatment: buprenorphine, methadone, or naltrexone. Carry naloxone, avoid using alone, and call 911 after an overdose.

NIDA treatment guidance

Methamphetamine & other stimulants

No FDA-approved medication currently treats stimulant use disorder. CBT, contingency management, and the Matrix Model have the strongest support.

ASAM stimulant guideline

Cocaine

Behavioral treatment—including CBT and contingency management—is the main evidence-based route. Ask a program whether it offers rewards-based contingency management.

NIDA treatment options

Cannabis

No approved medication currently treats cannabis use disorder. Motivational therapy, CBT, and practical sleep and trigger support can help with withdrawal and change.

NIDA cannabis guidance

Benzodiazepines

Do not stop abruptly after regular use. A clinician-supervised, gradual taper reduces dangerous withdrawal risk; the pace should be individualized.

ASAM tapering guideline

Nicotine

Free quit coaching plus medication works better than either alone. Call 1-800-QUIT-NOW; options include nicotine replacement, varenicline, and bupropion.

Call 1-800-QUIT-NOW · Free quit tools

Any substance

If abstinence is not possible today, reduce immediate risk: avoid mixing drugs, avoid using alone, use sterile supplies, test where available, and keep naloxone nearby.

Self-coaching

Build a plan you can use on a hard day

Write these answers somewhere private. Small, specific steps are easier to repeat than all-or-nothing promises.

01

Name the goal

Choose one next action: call a helpline, attend one meeting, ask about medication, remove supplies, or delay use for 20 minutes.

02

Map the trigger

Note the people, places, feelings, body sensations, money, and times of day that tend to come before use.

03

Plan the detour

Pair each trigger with one response: leave, call someone, eat, shower, walk, block a contact, attend a meeting, or use medication as prescribed.

04

Prepare for a lapse

A lapse is information, not failure. Reduce overdose risk, contact support, review what happened, and restart with the next safe choice.

Mental exercises

Skills for cravings and overwhelm

Urge surfing

Rate the urge 0–10. Notice where it sits in your body. Breathe and watch it rise, crest, and fall without arguing with it for 10 minutes.

HALT check

Ask: Am I hungry, angry, lonely, or tired? Meet the most basic need first, then reassess the urge.

Box breathing

Inhale for 4, hold for 4, exhale for 4, hold for 4. Repeat four rounds without forcing the breath.

5–4–3–2–1

Name 5 things you see, 4 you feel, 3 you hear, 2 you smell, and 1 you taste. Then choose one safe next action.

Challenge the thought

Write: What am I telling myself? What supports it? What does not? What would I tell a friend? What is a more balanced thought?

Daily check-in

Journal four lines: urge level, biggest trigger, one thing that helped, and the next 24-hour commitment. Keep it brief enough to repeat.

Open toolkit

Before you contact legal help

  1. Write a one-paragraph timeline: what happened, where, when, and who was involved.
  2. Keep the original notice, policy, bill, denial, medical record, email, or text.
  3. List every deadline and upcoming date at the top.
  4. Share only what the intake form asks for; do not send originals.
  5. Ask whether help is advice, representation, information, or a referral.

Review examines mental-health outcomes of affirming interventions

Privacy, without the fine-print fog

Your privacy deserves a soft landing.

TransGlowUp is here to help you find resources—not to build a profile about you. Here is the plain-language version of what happens when you visit.

Last updated: November 2, 2026.

What we collect

TransGlowUp does not offer accounts, ask you to sign in, or collect names, email addresses, health details, search entries, or transition-guide choices. We do not sell personal information.

Our hosting provider may create standard anonymous or aggregated traffic reports—such as page views, general device or browser type, approximate region, and referring page—to keep the site reliable and understand overall use. Those reports are not used by TransGlowUp to identify you.

Harm reduction, without judgment

  • With substances, avoid using alone; start low, go slowly, and avoid mixing depressants.
  • Carry naloxone where opioids may be present and use sterile supplies from a local program.
  • For binding or tucking, stop with pain, numbness, breathing trouble, skin injury, or overheating.
  • After harassment or violence, move toward a safer public place or trusted person when you can.

There is no single right way to do this, and there is no deadline. This guide gathers the practical things that helped other trans women early on — makeup that actually covers shadow, wigs that stay put, clothes that fit the body you have — plus the slower work of hormones, voice, and paperwork. Take what helps, skip what doesn't, and come back whenever you need a refresher. You deserve to feel good in your own skin, and small steps count.

Makeup how-tos

Neutralize beard shadow firstPress (don't rub) a peach or orange color corrector over the jaw and upper lip before foundation — it cancels the blue-gray undertone.
Match foundation to your jaw, not your wristTest three shades along the jawline in daylight. The one that disappears is yours.
Soften with contour, don't carveA cool-toned contour just under the cheekbones and along the jawline softens angularity. Blend upward.
Brows frame everythingBrow shape reads gender strongly. A softer arch with a slightly higher tail feminizes — get them professionally shaped once, then maintain.
One feature at a timeBold lip OR bold eye while you're learning, not both. A simple winged liner plus mascara goes a long way.
Setting spray is non-negotiableIt locks everything through heat, hugs, and long days.
Powder only where you shineA light dusting of translucent powder on the T-zone and jaw keeps makeup in place without dulling the rest of your face. Skip heavy powder on the cheeks — a little glow reads healthy and natural.
Blend corrector before it setsPeach corrector dries fast. Work one small area at a time — jaw, then upper lip — and tap foundation over it while it is still slightly tacky, so the layers melt together instead of sitting on top of each other.

Wig how-tos

Measure your headMost AMAB heads need average-large (22.5–23.5"). Measure around the hairline — too small means headaches and slipping.
Always wear a wig capA nude mesh cap flattens your hair and gives the wig grip. Tuck all bio hair in.
Synthetic is fine to startHeat-friendly synthetic wigs ($30–60) look great and hold their style. Human hair costs more and needs more upkeep.
Pluck the hairlineOut-of-the-box wigs have a harsh straight hairline. Tweezing a few hairs creates a natural gradient.
Secure it three waysAdjustable straps plus combs plus a little wig glue or tape at the temples — wind-proof confidence.
Wash cold, air dryCold water and wig shampoo, never wring. Dry on a stand to keep the shape.
Store it like you will wear it tomorrowA wig stand or a tall bottle keeps the shape between wears. Tossing it in a drawer tangles the fibers and flattens the style you worked for.
Match the part to your faceA side part softens a wider forehead; a middle part suits rounder faces. Most wigs let you shift the part a little — try both in the mirror before you commit to plucking or cutting.

Dressing for your body shape

Create a waistWrap dresses, peplum tops, and belts at the natural waist build an hourglass line on a straighter torso.
Balance broad shouldersA-line skirts, wide-leg pants, and V-necks draw the eye down and out. Skip shoulder pads and boat necks.
Longer torso? Go high-waistedHigh-rise jeans and skirts visually shorten the torso and lengthen the legs.
Shoe sizing: go up 1.5–2Women's sizes run about 1.5–2 smaller than men's — a men's 10 is roughly a women's 11.5–12. Look for wide widths.
Necklines matterScoop and V-necks soften a thicker neck. Chokers and short necklaces add femininity at the throat.
Tuck-friendly options existHigh-waisted bikini bottoms and one-pieces with good compression have you covered on swim days.
Dark bottoms, bright topA darker skirt or pant with a lighter top draws the eye up toward your face. It is a simple trick that works with almost everything you already own.
Fit the shoulders firstTops and dresses are hardest to alter at the shoulder. Buy to fit your shoulders, then tailor the waist — a small tuck at the tailor beats a closet full of almost-right.

Basic transitioning advice

HRT is a marathonEstrogen and blockers work over months and years, not weeks. Track changes with photos, not the day-to-day mirror. Talk to an affirming provider — see Find Care.
Voice feminization takes practicePitch is only part of it — resonance, intonation, and word choice matter more. Ten minutes of daily practice beats occasional long sessions.
Hair removal is a long gameLaser works best on dark hair plus light skin and takes 6–10 sessions. Electrolysis is permanent but slower. Start early if facial hair bothers you.
Social transition at your paceName, pronouns, presentation — in whatever order feels right. There is no required sequence.
Build your paper trail earlyResearch your state's name and gender-marker process now. Some steps take months.
Protect your peaceCurate your feed, find community (hi, that's us), and remember: you don't owe anyone an explanation.
Keep a little-wins listTransition has slow weeks. Write down the small wins — first good hair day, first time a stranger got your pronouns right — and read them back when the mirror is unkind.
Fertility is a before-HRT conversationIf you might want biological children someday, talk about sperm preservation before starting hormones. It is much harder — sometimes impossible — afterward, and clinics need lead time.

Transition 101

Trans Men

Hair, dressing for your body shape, and the basics — all in one place.

There is no single right way to do this, and there is no deadline. This guide gathers the practical things that helped other trans men early on — haircuts that read the way you want, binders that are safe and comfortable, clothes that fit the body you have — plus the slower work of testosterone, voice, and paperwork. Take what helps, skip what doesn't, and come back whenever you need a refresher. You deserve to feel good in your own skin, and small steps count. Your transition is yours alone: no one else gets to set the pace or the order.

Makeup how-tos

Think “no-makeup” makeupThe goal is even skin that doesn't look made up. Tinted moisturizer beats full foundation.
Sharpen with contourA warm-toned contour along the jawline and under the cheekbones adds angularity. Blend down, not up.
Thicken the browsFill sparse spots with short upward strokes. Straighter, thicker brows read masculine.
Kill the shine, keep the skinMatte powder on the T-zone. Dewy, glowy finishes read feminine.
Subtle beard stipplingA stipple sponge with brown cream pigment can suggest stubble shadow. Less is more — blend the edges.
Lips: balm onlyTinted or glossy lips read feminine. Plain balm keeps them healthy and neutral.
Color-match in daylightDrugstore lighting lies. Check your contour and brow shade by a window before you leave — what looks subtle inside can read orange or gray in the sun.
Exfoliate before you stippleStubble-look pigment grabs onto dry flakes and looks patchy. A gentle exfoliant plus moisturizer the night before gives the stipple sponge a smooth surface to work on.

Hair how-tos

Learn barber language“Tapered sides, textured top, squared neckline” gets you a masculine cut. Bring reference photos.
Short wigs exist tooMasculine-style short wigs and hairpieces work for pre-everything stages or performers — the same cap rules apply.
Style with matte productMatte clay or paste, not shiny gel. Work it through dry hair for texture.
Square the hairlineWhen trimming your own neckline, keep it straight and square — not rounded.
Surviving long hair pre-cutLow ponytail plus beanie, or ride out a masculine undercut grow-out. Hats are your friend in the awkward phase.
Facial hair: minoxidil is a personal choiceSome use it for beard growth. Research the risks and talk to a provider first.
Grow-out has stagesThe awkward in-between is real. A textured crop or a fade on the sides can carry you through months of grow-out without looking unfinished.
Ask for a consultation firstA good barber will talk through your face shape and hair texture before the clippers come out. If they rush you into the chair, that is a sign to try someone else.

Dressing for your body shape

Minimize the chestA properly fitted binder — never too small, max 8 hours — or a compression sports top. Layer with an open overshirt.
Binder safety is everythingRight size, take breaks, never sleep in it, never use tape or Ace bandages. Pain means take it off.
Broaden the topStructured jackets, flannels, and horizontal stripes up top balance wider hips.
Straighten the hip lineStraight-leg or relaxed-fit pants. Avoid skinny jeans and high-rise cuts that emphasize curves.
Layer like you mean itUndershirt plus overshirt or jacket adds bulk where you want it and hides what you don't.
Shoe sizingMen's sizes run bigger — a women's 10 is roughly a men's 8.5. The boys' and youth sections have smaller sizes and lower prices.
Cuffs and hems change everythingA quick hem on pants that pool at the ankle, and a single cuff on sleeves that swallow your hands, makes off-the-rack clothes look tailored.
Try a binder-friendly button-upShirts with a little stretch across the chest sit flatter over a binder than stiff cotton. Look for cotton-elastane blends, and size up if the buttons pull.

Basic transitioning advice

T works on its own timelineVoice drops, fat redistribution, and hair growth happen over months to years. Many changes are permanent — be sure before starting. Talk to an affirming provider.
Your voice will dropMost people's voices deepen significantly in the first year. Humming and speaking from the chest helps while you wait.
Top surgery is a processResearch surgeons early, know your insurance requirements, and line up aftercare help. Consults book months out.
Legal docs: pick your orderName change, then Social Security, then license and passport. Some states are easier than others.
Packing and STPs are optionalTotally personal. If you want to try, start cheap — nobody needs gear to be valid.
Find your guysTrans masc community spaces exist. You don't have to figure this out alone.
Voice changes need patienceYour voice will crack and wobble as it drops — that is normal, not damage. Avoid shouting through it, stay hydrated, and let the change settle over months.
Celebrate the firstsFirst time your voice cracks on a call, first time a stranger calls you sir — these moments land hard. Let yourself feel them. You worked for this, and noticing the changes is part of enjoying them.
Fertility is a before-T conversationIf biological children might be in your future, talk about egg preservation before starting testosterone. T can affect fertility, and preservation takes planning and time.

About TransGlowUp

TransGlowUp is a free hub for trans people and the people who love them — built by a trans woman who wanted one place where news, history, and gender-affirming care resources all live together, without a paywall in sight.

Too much of what trans people need is scattered, expensive, or buried in jargon. TransGlowUp gathers it in one readable place: daily trans news and history spotlights, step-by-step Transition 101 guides, a directory of verified clinicians, size converters, makeup tutorials, recovery resources, and a community Discord where nobody has to figure it out alone. The Glow Report podcast tells the same stories in audio, twice a week.

Everything here is free. There are no accounts, no sign-ins, and no subscriptions. The hub is kept alive by voluntary donations, affiliate links on a small set of recommended products (always labeled), and community merch — never by charging readers.

How listings are verified: clinicians in the directory are checked by phone and address before they are listed, and resource pages are reviewed against their original sources. If a listing is wrong or out of date, the contact page is the fastest way to flag it.

TransGlowUp is a community project, not a clinic, a law firm, or a crisis service. Its guides are educational, its crisis section points to professional help including 988, and its news summaries always credit the outlets they draw from.

Questions, corrections, or just want to say hi? The contact page and the Discord are both open.
