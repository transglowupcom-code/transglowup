The Pentagon Couldn't Name One Reason to Push Them Out — So a Federal Court Said the Troops Stay

On Tuesday, a federal appeals court in Washington, D.C., did something simple and, in this year, quietly extraordinary: it told the Pentagon it could not push 27 transgender service members out of the military while the government asks the Supreme Court to bless the ban. The reason, loves, is almost embarrassing in its plainness. After nearly two years of litigation, the government still cannot explain what harm keeping these troops in uniform would cause. The court finally said so out loud.

November 19, 2026 · 9 min read

What happened on Tuesday

On November 17, 2026, a three-judge panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit voted 2–1 to deny the Trump administration's request to keep its own June ruling on hold. The one-page order told the clerk to issue the court's mandate "forthwith" — immediately. That means the preliminary injunction first issued by a trial judge in March 2025, and upheld by the appeals court on June 1, 2026, is now actually in force for the first time since it was issued.

The case is Talbott v. USA, the first lawsuit filed against President Trump's January 2025 executive order targeting transgender service members and the Pentagon policy that followed it, known as the Hegseth Policy. The 27 service members covered by the order have together served more than 130 years and earned more than 80 commendations. The government has conceded that each of them serves honorably and meets every military standard. Even so, they had been forced onto administrative leave when they wanted to keep doing their jobs, and several had already been scheduled for separation boards — the military's process for pushing someone out.

Senior Judge Judith Rogers and Judge Robert Wilkins formed the majority. Judge Justin Walker would have granted the government's request. Judge Wilkins attached a seven-page statement explaining the vote, and it is worth reading closely, because it is the clearest statement yet of what this case is actually about.

The question the government still cannot answer

To keep a ruling on hold while it asks the Supreme Court to take a case, the government has to show it would suffer real, irreparable harm if the troops stayed in uniform. Judge Wilkins noted that the government never explained, at any stage of the case, what national security risk would come from keeping decorated service members who have met every military standard. A bare claim of executive authority, he wrote, is not enough: harm must be "certain and great," not theoretical.

He also rejected the government's argument that the Supreme Court's recent decision in West Virginia v. B.P.J. undermines the June ruling. The Hegseth Policy, he explained, sweeps in anyone who has ever "attempted to" transition — including by simply changing their clothing or pronouns — and so it targets people for being transgender, not for any medical condition. The government offered no explanation of how that connects to military readiness. "Stereotyping," he wrote, "is not a legitimate government interest."

That is the whole case in one paragraph, loves. The ban is not a medical policy. It is a policy that looks at a soldier who has served honorably for a decade, who has passed every test the military sets, and says: you cannot serve because of who you are. And when a court asks the government to justify that in the language of readiness and national security, there is nothing there. "Every time this administration has been asked to explain the Hegseth Policy, it has come up empty," said Jennifer Levi, senior director of transgender and queer rights at GLAD Law, one of the groups representing the troops. "The court saw this policy for what it is: a ban demeaning an entire group of people, dressed up in medical language."

Shannon Minter, legal director of the National Center for LGBTQ Rights, put it the same way: "The military's rule has always been that if you can do the job, you can serve; this ban throws that out and costs our armed forces proven, trained people. The government has never been able to identify any evidence to justify the ban, because there isn't any."

Who these troops are

It is easy to lose the people inside a case name, so here they are. The plaintiffs serve across all branches of the military, at some of the highest levels — a Major, a Captain, a Sergeant, a Navy pilot — with decades of experience between them, including a West Point education and several master's degrees. Their commendations range from Sailor of the Year and Meritorious Service Medals to Joint Service Commendations and a Bronze Star. Some come from decorated military families spanning generations, and they have served honorably around the country and the world on many deployments.

Second Lieutenant Nicolas Talbott, the Army officer whose name the case carries, said it plainly: "When you put on the uniform, differences fall away and what matters is your ability to do the job. Every individual must meet the same objective and rigorous qualifications in order to serve. It has been my dream and my goal to serve my country for as long as I can remember. My being transgender has no bearing on my dedication to the mission, my commitment to my unit, or my ability to perform my duties."

Sergeant First Class Kate Cole, who has spent more than half her life in the Army, including combat in Afghanistan, said removing qualified transgender soldiers "means an exodus of experienced personnel who fill key positions and can't be easily replaced, putting the burden on our fellow soldiers left behind. That's just wrong — and it destabilizes our armed forces."

These are not abstractions, loves. They are the people the policy was written to remove, and the people Tuesday's order lets keep serving.

The long road to Tuesday

Tuesday's order did not come out of nowhere. It is the latest turn in a case that has been running since the first week of the administration. On January 27, 2025, President Trump issued an executive order banning transgender people from serving in the U.S. military. The next day, GLAD Law and the National Center for LGBTQ Rights filed suit in federal court in Washington, D.C., on equal protection grounds. On February 26, 2025, the Pentagon released the policy carrying it out — a policy that, in the government's own words, "generally disqualifies from military service individuals who have gender dysphoria, a history of the condition, or a history of related interventions."

On March 18, 2025, Judge Ana Reyes of the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia issued a nationwide preliminary injunction, halting the ban. In her ruling, she noted that "thousands of transgender servicemembers have sacrificed — some risking their lives — to ensure for others the very equal protection rights the Military Ban seeks to deny them." But the D.C. Circuit put that order on hold while it considered the government's appeal, and on May 6, 2025, the Supreme Court issued a ruling in a separate case, Shilling v. Trump, allowing the ban to take effect while the legal challenges moved forward.

That is why Tuesday matters so much: the injunction protecting these troops has existed on paper since March 2025, but it has been frozen for more than a year and a half. On June 1, 2026, the D.C. Circuit panel upheld the injunction as to the currently serving plaintiffs, concluding that the Hegseth Policy likely violates equal protection because it is not rationally related to any legitimate government interest. The panel vacated the injunction as to the plaintiffs seeking to enlist. The government then asked the court to keep that ruling on hold while it sought Supreme Court review — and on Tuesday, the court said no.

On November 2, the troops' lawyers filed their opposition to the government's Supreme Court petition, arguing there is nothing for the justices to review yet: the ruling is a narrow preliminary order, and a full trial in the case is scheduled to begin in federal district court in January 2027.

What Tuesday does not do

Now the honest part, because a win this narrow deserves to be described exactly. Tuesday's order protects only the 27 named plaintiffs who are already serving. It does not extend to people seeking to enlist, and it does not strike down the ban. The Hegseth Policy remains in force across the armed forces, and the Pentagon has continued separating other transgender troops while this case has moved through the courts. The exact number pushed out is not publicly known — and that is its own quiet scandal, that a purge this large can proceed without a public count.

The Supreme Court is the next step. The government's petition asking the justices to review the June decision — United States v. Talbott, No. 26-272 — was distributed on November 17 for the justices' private conference on December 4, 2026. At that conference, the justices will decide whether to take the case. No decision accepting review has been announced, and the troops' lawyers argue the Court should wait for the January trial.

There is also a class-action question still open. In April 2026, the plaintiffs asked Judge Reyes to certify the case as a class action on behalf of all transgender service members, so that any ruling would apply to everyone affected by the ban, not just the named plaintiffs. A hearing on that request was held on June 30, 2026. If the class is certified, Tuesday's protection could eventually reach far beyond 27 people. If it is not, the ban keeps standing for everyone outside this case.

So celebrate this one — it is real, and it is the first time in more than a year and a half that a court order has actually shielded these troops from discharge. But do not mistake it for the end. Twenty-seven people can keep their uniforms today. Thousands of others still cannot put one on, or are still being pushed out of the one they already wear.

Why this matters on the last day of the week

Loves, today is the final day of Transgender Awareness Week, and tomorrow is Transgender Day of Remembrance — the day we say the names of the trans people we lost this year. This story is the week in miniature, the same pattern we have watched all seven days: a real, measurable gain sitting right next to a real, measurable harm, and a community holding both at once.

The gain is this: a federal court looked at the government's own record, found nothing in it, and said the troops stay. The harm is this: the ban is still the law for everyone else, and the Supreme Court may yet decide to take the case. The week gave us Australia's top health body backing puberty blockers, a federal appeals court hearing the defence of Colorado's Kelly Loving Act, Delhi opening its hospitals to trans families — and, alongside all of it, the bans, the medical boards, the voter rolls quietly shrinking.

Both things are true at once. The work of this week — and of tomorrow, when we remember the dead — is holding both of them: celebrating the doors that opened, and refusing to look away from the ones that are still locked. Twenty-seven decorated troops get to keep serving tonight because a judge asked the government for one reason and got silence. That silence is the story. And on December 4, the Supreme Court will decide whether it wants to hear more of it.

What you can do next: Follow Talbott v. USA through the groups representing the troops — GLAD Law and the National Center for LGBTQ Rights both publish case updates — and mark December 4, when the Supreme Court decides whether to take the case. If you are a service member affected by the ban, both organisations have information on the case and on your options. And if you know a trans person in uniform, tell them about Tuesday: for 27 of their own, the court finally said they can stay.
