Paxton Just Dropped His Lawsuits Against the Texas Doctors Who Treated Trans Kids — as Voters Show Him the Door

For two years, Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton held two pediatricians up as villains — calling them "scofflaws" and "radical gender activists" in press releases, threatening them with steep fines and the loss of their medical licenses for treating trans teenagers. On Friday, November 20, he quietly dropped both cases. No trial. No verdict. No explanation. Just a notice of non-suit filed in a Collin County courtroom, weeks after Texas voters ended his Senate campaign and handed his office to a Democrat.

November 25, 2026 · 8 min read

What happened on Friday

Loves, here is the short version. Paxton's office filed notices of non-suit — the legal term for voluntarily dropping a case — in Collin County district court on November 20, ending the state's lawsuits against Dr. M. Brett Cooper, an assistant professor of pediatrics at the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center, and Dr. May Lau, a former adolescent medicine physician at Children's Medical Center Dallas. The cases were dismissed Friday, according to attorneys for both doctors. Neither case ever reached a trial or a verdict.

Both suits were brought under Senate Bill 14, the 2023 Texas law that bars doctors from prescribing puberty blockers or gender-transition hormones to minors. Texas was the first state in the country to bring cases against individual doctors under a youth care ban — and now all three of the doctors Paxton sued have seen their cases dropped.

Paxton's office did not immediately return a request for comment when the Associated Press asked about the dismissals on Monday.

Who these doctors are — and what Paxton accused them of

Dr. Cooper's case was filed in November 2024. Paxton's office accused him of using "false, misleading, and deceptive acts and practices to mislead pharmacies, insurance providers, and/or patients" — specifically, of "falsifying medical records, prescriptions, and billing records to conceal that his treatments and prescriptions are for the unlawful purposes of transitioning a child's biological sex." Cooper has always denied wrongdoing. His attorneys, Simona Agnolucci and Anika Holland, said in a statement that he "has always maintained that he acted lawfully and in the best interests of his patients."

Dr. Lau's case was the nation's first lawsuit against a doctor for providing gender-affirming care to trans youth. Paxton accused her of authorizing testosterone for at least 21 teenagers to transition or affirm their gender identity — an accusation she denies. The lawsuit cost her enormously even without a trial: she surrendered her Texas medical license in 2025 because of the case, and moved her practice from Dallas to Oregon.

Her attorney, Nicole LeBoeuf, did not mince words in a statement: "Dr. Lau did nothing wrong, but she was a convenient scapegoat for the Attorney General's Office, which issued press release after press release about its case — a case that has now collapsed." LeBoeuf said the timing of the dismissal, coming right after Paxton's election loss, "validates that this case against Dr. Lau was political from beginning to end."

That last point is worth sitting with. These cases were never really about what happened in an exam room. They were press releases with a lawsuit attached — a way for an attorney general to build a national profile as a conservative fighter by putting doctors' names and licenses on the line. The cases made headlines when they were filed. They ended with a quiet court filing on a Friday.

The third doctor — and the pattern that is now complete

Cooper and Lau were the second and third doctors Paxton sued. The first was Dr. Hector Granados, an El Paso pediatric endocrinologist who was among the first doctors in the nation accused of illegally providing transition care to minors. Paxton's suit against Granados called him a "scofflaw who is harming the health and safety of Texas children by providing puberty blockers and cross-sex hormones to children." The state dropped that case in September 2025 — and Paxton's office later said Granados did not break the law.

So the full record of Paxton's doctor prosecutions is now this: three doctors sued, three cases dropped, zero verdicts, zero findings that anyone broke the law. One doctor lost her Texas license anyway. One moved her practice to another state. All three spent years under the threat of fines and professional ruin.

That is the part of the story that does not get undone by a non-suit. A dropped case is not an apology, and it does not give Dr. Lau her Texas license back or return the years she spent fighting. What it does do is end the threat — and it tells every other doctor in Texas watching this that the state's most aggressive enforcement campaign against youth care providers has collapsed on its own terms.

Why now: the voters, the exit, and the pile of dropped cases

The timing is not a mystery. Paxton lost his bid for the U.S. Senate to Democrat James Talarico on November 3, and he is now in his final weeks in office. In January, Democrat Nathan Johnson — a state senator from Dallas — will become Texas' next attorney general, one of several Democrats who won back statewide offices from Republicans on November 3, including the governorship.

The doctor suits are only the latest in a series of cases Paxton's office has dropped since the election. The list includes a lawsuit against the NCAA over transgender athletes in women's sports, dropped about a week earlier; lawsuits against out-of-state doctors accused of mailing abortion pills to Texans; a suit seeking to shut down Annunciation House, one of the oldest migrant shelters on the U.S.-Mexico border; a suit against ActBlue, the Democratic fundraising platform; a suit against the Jolt Initiative, a nonprofit accused of registering noncitizens to vote; and a suit against the Council on American-Islamic Relations, the nation's largest Muslim civil rights group, which outgoing Governor Greg Abbott had declared a terrorist organization.

It is a remarkable wind-down: the outgoing attorney general is spending his last weeks in office dismantling the very cases he used to define his tenure. The pattern is hard to miss — cases that were useful as campaign material are being quietly abandoned now that there is no campaign left to run.

The ads that didn't work

The election that ended Paxton's Senate run was also the most expensive anti-trans advertising blitz in American history, and Texas was its epicenter. Republicans spent an estimated $183 million on television ads focused on transgender-related attacks against Democrats in this year's midterms, according to tallies from data firm AdImpact shared with the Associated Press — and the Texas Senate contest between Paxton and Talarico consumed the largest share of that spending, drawing an estimated $58 million for more than 50,000 airings before the election.

We covered the national picture here on November 13: voters told pollsters the obsession was "strange and over the top," and the candidates targeted by the ads mostly won. Texas proved the point at the top of the ticket. Paxton, a close ally of President Donald Trump who had built his brand on these fights, lost — and the lawsuits he filed to burnish that brand are being dropped before he leaves office.

That does not mean the ads did no damage. Fifty-eight million dollars of anti-trans messaging in one state leaves a mark on public conversation, on families, and on kids who heard themselves talked about as a threat on every commercial break. But as a political strategy, it failed in the place where it was tried hardest. The voters heard the message, and then they voted for the other guy.

Meanwhile in Massachusetts: a hospital that stopped care is starting it again

Texas is not the only place where the ground is shifting this week. On Tuesday, November 24, Baystate Health — the largest health system in Western Massachusetts — announced it will resume gender-affirming care for young people, nine months after it stopped prescribing hormone medications and puberty blockers to patients under 18.

Baystate halted the care in February 2026, citing an evolving regulatory landscape that threatened hundreds of millions of dollars in Medicaid and Medicare funding. The system said it will resume scheduling appointments now, and will again prescribe gender-affirming medication for children starting December 15. The program includes medical and psychological evaluations, counseling, and medications.

Massachusetts Attorney General Andrea Campbell said her office worked for months with providers and advocates to get the care restored. "I'm glad Baystate has decided to resume providing gender-affirming care to young people," she said in a statement, "which never should have been suspended in the first place." She added that "Baystate has a responsibility to build back trust with the families and communities they serve."

Campbell's office had issued a legal advisory in May stating that federal law does not prohibit providers from offering the care, and that Massachusetts law prohibits discrimination in health care facilities on the basis of gender identity. Baystate serves about 800,000 people across Western Massachusetts, so the reversal matters far beyond one hospital — and it is a reminder that the hospital pullbacks we have watched all year were choices made under pressure, not legal requirements. Where an attorney general pushes back, care comes back.

What this means for trans health — and what it doesn't

Let's be honest about the limits, because this post will not oversell a win. Dropping these two lawsuits does not change Senate Bill 14. The ban on gender-affirming care for trans minors in Texas is still the law, and it still stands between Texas families and the care their kids need. A doctor who prescribes hormones to a minor in Texas today still faces the same statute Paxton used to sue Cooper and Lau.

But the repeal effort is already moving. As we covered here on November 14, Rep. Donna Howard filed HB 126 on the first day of bill filing — November 9 — to repeal SB 14's youth care ban, and the legislative session begins January 12, 2027. The new attorney general taking office in January will not be the one who built his career on these prosecutions. And the message to doctors is real: the state's own cases against the three providers it chose to make examples of all ended with nothing.

The bigger picture is the one we keep seeing this month. In Michigan, the state reversed its plan to cut Medicaid coverage for trans kids and decided to pay for it with state money instead. In Massachusetts, a hospital system that stopped youth care under federal pressure is restarting it. In Texas, the attorney general who sued the doctors is dropping the cases on his way out the door. None of these is a final victory. All of them point the same direction: the enforcement campaigns are running out of road, and the care is coming back where people fight for it.

For the families in Texas who watched their children's doctors get sued for doing their jobs — and for the doctors themselves — this week brought something they have not had in two years: the end of a threat. That is not everything. But after two years of press releases and court filings, it is something real. And it happened because voters, in Texas of all places, decided they had heard enough.

What to watch next: Nathan Johnson takes over as Texas attorney general in January 2027, and the legislative session opens January 12 — watch whether HB 126, the bill to repeal the youth care ban, gets a hearing. In Massachusetts, Baystate Health resumes prescribing gender-affirming medication to young people on December 15. And watch whether other hospital systems that paused youth care under federal pressure follow Baystate's lead now that the ground is shifting.
