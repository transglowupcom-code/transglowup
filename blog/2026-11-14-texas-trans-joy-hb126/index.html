Unbridled Joy in Texas: Trans Texans Are Planning a Future Again — and the Repeal Bills Are Already Filed

It is day two of Transgender Awareness Week, and in Texas something is happening that trans Texans have not felt in years. On election night, more than a hundred people gathered outside the state Capitol in Austin — cheering, dancing, waving at honking drivers — to celebrate wins they had been told were impossible. And within days, the very first bills filed for the 2027 legislative session included one to repeal the ban on gender-affirming care for trans youth. Hope, for once, has paperwork behind it.

November 14, 2026 · 6 min read

"Unbridled joy"

Loves, start with the feeling, because the feeling is the news. Joelle Espeut, a transgender woman and advocacy director for the Normal Anomaly Initiative, told the Texas Tribune she felt "unbridled joy" as the results came in. "It's been pretty grim in Texas the last several years," she said. "It's been this ongoing anti-trans and anti-LGBT rhetoric. I think what Tuesday brought was hope that things can change after several years of literally fighting for our lives."

That is not a small thing to say out loud in a state where trans people have spent years playing defense — against a ban on gender-affirming care for minors, restrictions on bathroom use and gender markers on identification documents, and bans on pride groups in public schools. The election changed the shape of the fight. Democrats now hold the governor's office, the House, the lieutenant governor's office, and the attorney general's office, along with a significant share of the Texas Supreme Court. Sen.-elect James Talarico and Gov.-elect Gina Hinojosa won their races on the same night trans Texans were celebrating outside the Capitol.

For 26-year-old Alma Zamora, an Austin resident and Rio Grande Valley native who joined that crowd of more than a hundred people, the shift was not abstract at all. It was about whether she could imagine a future in the city she has lived in for eight years. "I never thought I'd ever be able to own a home in Austin," she said. "I think with this new government, we have an option. I can start thinking ahead. I can think to the future." That is what a political win sounds like when it lands on a real person: not a slogan, but a plan — a home, a future, a life you can think ahead to.

Andrea Segovia, deputy director for the Transgender Education Network of Texas, put the strategic shift plainly. For years, she said, trans advocacy in Texas has been defensive — "let's hold the line, let's not make things worse." After the election, she told her team: "this is our moment." The work now is not only stopping the next ban. It is asking the people who just won to deliver. "We gave them this opportunity as voters to be in these high positions of power, and now we have to make sure that they deliver on promises and beliefs that they had," Segovia said. "I think that we should push our champions to be champions."

$144 million, and it didn't work

The joy in Austin was earned the hard way. Republican campaigns spent nearly $144 million in Texas on midterm ads that described Democrats as unfit for office and out of step with Texas based on their support for transgender people or opposition to GOP-led restrictions on trans Texans, according to data from the ad-tracking firm AdImpact. The messaging was sharply focused on trans people, particularly trans youth — with ads and conservative pundits frequently attacking Talarico for his opposition to bills restricting gender-affirming care for minors. We covered the national version of this story yesterday: more than $400 million spent across the country, and voters calling the obsession "strange and over the top." Texas was the most expensive front of that campaign.

Espeut believes the ads fell short because the message had gone stale — and because it ignored what voters were actually worried about. "When you focus on this anti-trans rhetoric, it doesn't land because everyone is going through or is impacted by the current state of the economy, and people are just not interested in uplifting this hate," she said. Segovia made the same point with a sharper edge: "All of this money is going towards this, and people can't afford a loaf of bread." Attack ads about trans kids, in other words, do not pay the rent. Voters noticed.

There is a lesson in that for the community, too — one Espeut was careful to name. If Democrats want to improve trans Texans' lives, she said, they should start by addressing the issues that affect all Texans: housing, healthcare, and public safety. "Trans issues," she said, "are often the same as for any other Texan." Of course the community wants the harm undone, she added — but "by centering social issues that impact all Texans, everyone will be uplifted." That is not a retreat from trans rights. It is the case for them, made in the language of a shared life: the same rent, the same doctor's bill, the same unsafe street.

The repeal bill is already filed

Here is where the hope turns into something you can look up. Monday, November 9 — the first day Texas lawmakers could pre-file bills for the 2027 session — one of the first bills through the door was House Bill 126, filed by Rep. Donna Howard, an Austin Democrat. It would repeal two of the laws that defined the red-state era in Texas: the state's abortion ban and its ban on gender-affirming care for trans youth.

The trans care provisions are specific. HB 126 would repeal Subchapter X of Chapter 161 of the Texas Health and Safety Code — the core of SB 14, the 2023 law that bars doctors from providing puberty blockers, hormone therapy, and surgical care to transgender youth. It would also repeal the Medicaid exclusion from SB 14, the provision that cut off public funding for that care. USA Today confirmed the same scope in its own reporting on the filings: Howard's bill would repeal the state's 2023 ban on gender-transition care for minors, alongside the state's three abortion bans — the 2022 trigger ban, the 2021 SB 8 heartbeat law, and the 1925 pre-Roe ban.

It was not the only bill. As bill filing opened this week, Democratic lawmakers also submitted legislation to increase penalties for crimes influenced by a victim's sexual orientation or gender identity, and to establish prohibitions on discrimination based on gender and sexual orientation. Other Democrats filed additional repeal bills, including House Bill 948 from Rep.-elect Samantha Lopez-Resendez of Austin and House Bill 987 from Rep. Rhetta Bowers of Garland. In total, lawmakers filed more than 1,260 bills on the first day of pre-filing alone. The session itself begins January 12, 2027, and pre-filing continues through March 12.

The honest other side

Loves, we will not pretend this bill is about to become law, because it almost certainly is not — not yet. Democrats did not flip the Texas Senate. It remains Republican-controlled, and HB 126 will likely not get past it. Erin Reed, who first reported the bill's details, called it a messaging bill — and she is right. It is also a significant one. A bill like this forces a vote, puts every senator on the record, and tells trans families in Texas that their care is worth fighting for in the legislature, not just defending in court.

There is also a gap in the bill worth knowing about, because the details matter when a family's care is on the line. HB 126 repeals one of the two provisions that would cost doctors their licenses for providing trans youth care — Section 164.052(a)(24), which lists trans youth care among a doctor's prohibited practices — but it does not repeal Section 164.0552, which requires the Texas Medical Board to revoke the license of any doctor who provides gender-affirming care to transgender youth. That omission may be an oversight, or it may be fixed in a later draft. Either way, it is the kind of detail advocates will be watching as the session approaches — because a repeal that leaves the license threat in place is not a full repeal.

And the harm the bills aim to undo is not hypothetical. Texas' ban has already cost trans kids access to the care their doctors said they needed, driven families out of the state, and created a hostile environment for the families who stayed. Undoing that will take more than one bill, more than one session, and more than one election. The Senate is the wall. The work is the wall coming down, piece by piece, and this week was the first piece.

Why this is the right story for day two of Transgender Awareness Week

Transgender Awareness Week runs November 13 through November 19, leading into Transgender Day of Remembrance on November 20. The week exists so that more people can know trans people — and the evidence keeps saying that knowing trans people is what changes minds. The Texas story is that evidence in its purest form: a state where $144 million in attack ads failed, where voters chose candidates who stood with trans people, and where the first legislative act of the new era was a bill to give trans kids their care back.

Espeut's closing thought is the one to carry into the rest of the week. "There is so much that needs to be undone and changed, and that hopefully will happen in time," she said. "But I really think that the biggest thing is just to make sure that we are still doing things collectively, and with us moving forward that we aren't forgetting any part of the community, trans Texans or anybody." The joy in Austin was real. The bills are filed. And nobody gets left behind on the way to the future Zamora is finally letting herself plan for.
