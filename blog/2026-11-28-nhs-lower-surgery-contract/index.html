One Hospital Left: England's Trans Masc Lower Surgeries Lose a Provider on Monday — and the Wait Is Already Eight Years

On Monday, November 30, the New Victoria Hospital in London stops performing masculinising genital surgery for the NHS. From December, there will be just one hospital in the whole country offering these operations on the NHS — and people referred now can expect to wait around eight years.

November 28, 2026 · 6 min read

What is happening on Monday

Loves, here is the situation in plain words. There are two hospitals in the UK that carry out masculinising genital surgeries — the lower surgeries trans men and trans masculine people wait years for. One of them, the New Victoria Hospital, a private hospital in London that was acquired by Bupa in 2025, declined to renew its NHS contract to deliver the surgeries in May this year. It told NHS England on May 28. Its contract ends on November 30, and from December 1, the Chelsea and Westminster Hospital — also in London — will be the only NHS provider of these surgeries in the entire country.

The NHS has not yet found a new provider to take on the contract. That is the part that should make everyone sit up, because hundreds of trans patients are referred for these surgeries every year, and the waiting list was already around five years long. People referred now, according to reporting by QueerAF published today, can expect to wait around eight years.

The surgeries at stake are metoidioplasty, which uses existing genital tissue to create a penis, and phalloplasty, which creates a penis using tissue from elsewhere on the body. Both can be multi-stage surgeries, with phalloplasty involving at least three operations spread over several years. These are not cosmetic procedures. For the people waiting for them, they are the surgeries that let their bodies match who they are — and the wait for them shapes years of a person's life: time off work, childcare arrangements, mental preparation, and the simple fact of living in a body that does not feel like your own while the calendar crawls.

What happens to the people already waiting

The NHS says patients will be transferred to Chelsea and Westminster, and that those already referred will keep their original referral date and place on the waiting list. Andrew Fairweather, the chief executive of New Victoria Hospital, confirmed to QueerAF that patients can continue to access care through Chelsea and Westminster NHS Foundation Trust from December 1, and that a number of the hospital's staff who have been caring for these patients will move to Chelsea and Westminster too, to keep some continuity and familiarity of care. The hospital says it has been working closely with NHS England to ensure a smooth handover.

The NHS Gender Dysphoria National Referral Support Service has published a page of answers for affected patients. It confirms that New Victoria Hospital will not deliver masculinising genital surgery after November 30, that referrals will stay with the national referral service, and that NHS England is in discussions with providers about what happens next. It also says, honestly, that it has no more information and no timescale for further updates. Chelsea and Westminster continues to provide the surgery, and patients who have waited longest are being offered its service first.

But the reporting from patients tells a harder story than the official handover language. Derek, whose name has been changed, had his second consultation for lower surgery in October 2025 and was one step away from an operation. After a ten-month wait without any communication from the NHS, a letter arrived in August — not with a surgery date, but telling him his surgery was cancelled. He told QueerAF the letter led him to believe New Victoria was the only provider of this surgery on the NHS. "I had a full-on dissociative episode. I was good and shook for a couple of weeks there," he said. "It still feels like a weight on my chest. I feel more anxious." He has had no further communication from anyone involved in his care since, and no reply from his NHS gender clinic when he asked what his options are.

Then there is Chris, also a changed name, who was first referred for lower surgery in 2019 and had his first-stage metoidioplasty seven years later, in September 2026, at New Victoria Hospital. He did not know the hospital was about to stop performing the surgeries, and expected his next operation to be with the same surgeon and team. He learned about the contract ending from a Facebook group. He has now been transferred to Chelsea and Westminster for his follow-up appointments and the second stage of his surgery — against his wishes. He says he would have refused the transfer, holding out hope his surgeon would begin practising elsewhere, if it were not for the risk that refusing would send him to the bottom of the waiting list. "Exhausting, frustrating and, at times, scary" is how he describes the experience. After surgery he developed an infection and could not reach the nurses assigned to his care — one replied with an out-of-office message, the other did not reply at all — and he ended up at a walk-in health centre, where a doctor who had never treated a patient who had undergone metoidioplasty prescribed antibiotics and urged him to get support from his surgical team.

These are individual stories, and they are not statistics. But they show what a contract decision looks like from the other side: not a line in a procurement document, but a letter that cancels a surgery you have waited years for, and a transfer to a different surgeon halfway through a multi-stage procedure, with no real choice in the matter.

This has happened before — and the backlog never cleared

The most alarming part of this story is that it is not the first time. In March 2020, the NHS did not recommission the former provider, St Peter's Andrology Centre, to deliver these surgeries. The result was an eighteen-month period, between 2020 and 2021, when there was no NHS provision for masculinising genital surgeries at all. Not a longer wait — no provision. People were left stuck between stages of surgery, or unable to have complications addressed, for a year and a half. New Victoria Hospital took on the contract in September 2021, and the gap "created a huge backlog," according to legal action being brought by the law firm Leigh Day on behalf of patients who were abandoned by the NHS part way through a complex surgical process.

That history matters, because some of the people waiting for a surgery date now were also waiting during that eighteen-month gap. The same waiting list, the same limbo, twice. QueerAF's analysis puts it plainly: because these surgeries require long stretches of time off work, relying on others to care for you, and mental and emotional preparation — and because the procedures involve multiple operations spread over several years — there is an extra layer of anxiety about providers changing and leaving patients stranded between stages. And the problem reaches beyond people still waiting for surgery: patients who have already had lower surgery may face complications years down the line, and their original surgical team is no longer there to resolve them.

Chay Brown, healthcare spokesperson for TransActual, told QueerAF that the decision has "delayed care for trans patients who have already spent years on waiting lists." He added that while NHS England could not control the decision made by New Victoria Hospital, "they do still have a responsibility to minimise the harm of delayed care."

One option the NHS could use — and the wider picture

There is one route that could ease the wait, and it is worth knowing about. Brown points out that the NHS could reimburse trans patients to access lower surgery abroad, through the Planned Treatment Scheme — the S2 funding route. "Where patients face undue delays to healthcare that is routinely available with NHS England, the healthcare provider may support patients to access care abroad — in EU countries or beyond — rather than delay further," he said. TransActual is urging NHS England to consider that option for the patients affected while it looks for an alternative provider. The NHS referral service's own FAQ points patients to its guidance on going abroad for medical treatment, and says NHS England is in discussions with providers about next steps.

The wider picture in the UK is not encouraging. On top of the staggering waiting times for NHS gender clinics and the restrictions on care for trans youth, the Welsh Gender Service has also paused all new referrals and existing scheduled surgeries for patients in Wales, pending the outcome of an independent review of the service — a pause announced on August 28, 2026. Patients referred by a gender clinic from England, Scotland or Northern Ireland are not affected by the Welsh pause, but for Welsh patients the door is closed for now, with no date given for it to reopen.

It is also worth being honest about what we do not know. We do not know how many patients New Victoria Hospital was treating, or how many had operations cancelled. We do not know when — or whether — NHS England will find a replacement provider, and the referral service says it has no timescale. We do not know how much longer the wait will get with one hospital doing the work of two. What we do know is that the list was already around five years long, that hundreds of people join it every year, and that from Monday the whole country's NHS provision for these surgeries rests on a single hospital in London.

What this means, here at home

Most of the people reading this blog live in the United States, not the UK. So why does a hospital contract in London matter here? Because the pattern is the same one we keep writing about: care for trans people treated as something a system can pause, hand off, or quietly let lapse — and patients left to carry the consequences alone. In Michigan, we saw a state reverse course and keep paying for care. In Massachusetts, a federal judge struck down the ban on trans HIV care. In the UK this week, we see the other direction: a provider walks away, no replacement is ready, and the people who waited seven years for a first operation are told to start over with a new surgeon or lose their place in line.

The lesson travels. When care depends on a single provider, a single contract, or a single funding line, it is fragile — and the people who depend on it are the ones who find out first. The fix is the same everywhere: more providers, not fewer; funding that survives a contract ending; and a system that treats a waiting list as people with lives on hold, not numbers on a spreadsheet. The patients in this story waited years for letters that told them their surgery was cancelled. They deserved better than a form letter and silence. So does everyone still waiting — in London, in Wales, and here at home.

What you can do next: If you are in the UK and affected by this change, the NHS Gender Dysphoria National Referral Support Service has a page of answers for New Victoria Hospital patients, and your gender clinic can advise on your referral. And if you are in the US and in an emergency situation, the Trans Safety Emergency Fund reopens for applications on Monday, December 1, and closes December 3 — it offers direct help with housing, health and safety costs for trans, nonbinary and gender-diverse people.
