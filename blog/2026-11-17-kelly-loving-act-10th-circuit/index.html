A Law Named for a Club Q Victim Went Before a Federal Appeals Court Yesterday

On Monday, November 16, 2026, a federal appeals court in Denver heard arguments over Colorado's Kelly Loving Act — the law that says a trans person's chosen name and pronouns are part of who they are under the state's anti-discrimination law. The judges didn't rule. They asked a narrower question first: do the businesses challenging the law even face a real threat of being punished under it?

November 17, 2026 · 8 min read

What happened yesterday

Loves, this one is about a name — and about whether the law gets to protect it. Yesterday morning, a three-judge panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 10th Circuit heard oral arguments in three related appeals challenging Colorado's Kelly Loving Act, in the Byron White U.S. Courthouse in Denver. The hearing lasted into the morning, the judges took the appeals under consideration, and no ruling date was announced.

The challengers are two businesses, represented by the Alliance Defending Freedom: XX-XY Athletics, a Denver athletic apparel company founded by Jennifer Sey, and Born Again Used Books, a Christian bookstore in Colorado Springs run by Eric and Sara Smith. Medical professionals and advocacy groups are parties to the related appeals as well. All of them are asking the court to temporarily block the law while their lawsuits move forward — and all of them were turned down on that request once already, by a federal district judge in March.

That is why the hearing yesterday was, in a sense, about the hearing itself: the panel spent much of its time on whether the challengers face a credible threat of enforcement. That question decides whether they even get to ask a court to block the law before the state takes any action against them. Colorado attorney Janna Fischer told the judges they don't — that the challengers' proposed conduct does not actually violate the law, so there is nothing to be punished for. The businesses' lawyers argued the opposite: that the threat of investigations, hearings, and penalties is real enough that they are already changing how they speak.

What the law actually does

Here is the part that gets lost when this story is reduced to a fight about "pronouns." The Kelly Loving Act — House Bill 25-1312 — was signed by Governor Jared Polis in May 2025. It amended the Colorado Anti-Discrimination Act to define "gender expression" as including a person's "chosen name" and "how the individual chooses to be addressed." In practice, that means a business open to the public cannot deny a trans person the full and equal enjoyment of its goods and services because of their gender identity or expression — and deliberately misusing a person's preferred name, form of address, or gender-related pronoun can count as harassment that creates a hostile environment.

The law is named for Kelly Loving, a transgender woman who was killed in the 2022 mass shooting at Club Q, the LGBTQ nightclub in Colorado Springs. She was one of five people murdered that night. Naming the law for her was a deliberate choice: it says that the dignity of trans people in public life — being called by your name, in a shop, at a counter, in a waiting room — is not a courtesy. It is a civil right, and it is worth a law.

That is what is on the table in this case. Not a slogan, not a culture-war abstraction — a trans person walking into a store and being addressed as themselves, and whether the state can say that matters.

The honest part: what the other side says, and what the courts have said so far

A trustworthy blog has to tell you the other side's argument straight, even when we disagree with it. The challengers say the law compels speech: that it forces them to use language they believe is false, in violation of the First Amendment. The Alliance Defending Freedom argues the law exposes businesses to investigations, hearings, and civil and criminal penalties — including, by ADF's account, up to 120 days in jail for violations of the law's discriminatory-advertising provisions. The Smiths, who run the bookstore, have said they could face a $3,500 fine for not using a person's preferred name or pronouns.

Now here is what the courts have actually done with those arguments so far. In March 2026, U.S. District Judge Regina Rodriguez denied the challengers' requests to temporarily block enforcement of the law, finding they had not shown a credible threat of it being enforced against them. In September 2026, she refused to dismiss the businesses' claims outright, finding that their intended conduct is "arguably proscribed" by the state's anti-discrimination law — meaning the case is real enough to proceed, even if the threat of enforcement was not strong enough to justify blocking the law in advance. In October, she paused the district court case until the appeal and the state's request to send the question of the law's scope to the Colorado Supreme Court are both resolved.

So the picture is genuinely mixed, and we are not going to pretend otherwise. A judge has said the challengers' claims are arguable, and the same judge has said they have not shown they are about to be punished. Yesterday's panel — which included U.S. District Judge Kelly Rankin of Wyoming, sitting by designation — did not rule from the bench. It will decide, first, whether the businesses can even get a preliminary injunction while the case goes on. If they can't, the law stays in effect and the case continues in the ordinary way. If they can, the fight moves to the bigger question: whether the First Amendment shields a business's refusal to use a trans person's name and pronouns.

Why this hearing matters beyond Colorado

Colorado has become the testing ground for this exact argument, again and again. In 2023, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled against Colorado in 303 Creative LLC v. Elenis, holding that the state could not compel a website designer to create expressive work conveying messages she opposed. The businesses challenging the Kelly Loving Act are building on that ruling: if the state cannot compel a designer to make a website, they argue, it cannot compel a shopkeeper to use a name.

And earlier this year, the Supreme Court decided another Colorado case on the same fault line. On March 31, 2026, in Chiles v. Salazar, the Court ruled 8–1 that Colorado's ban on conversion therapy for minors, as applied to talk therapy, regulates speech based on viewpoint and therefore has to survive strict scrutiny — the toughest test in constitutional law. Justice Neil Gorsuch wrote the majority opinion; Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson dissented. The Court reversed the 10th Circuit, which had upheld the ban, and sent the case back. The ruling did not strike down every conversion therapy ban — it sent the case back for a new look under the stricter standard — but it made one thing clear: when a law touches speech, even speech in a professional setting, the courts will look at it very hard.

That is the legal weather this case is walking into. The same court that heard yesterday's arguments once upheld Colorado's conversion therapy ban, and the Supreme Court reversed it. The same First Amendment arguments are now being run against a law that protects trans people's names. How the 10th Circuit handles the standing question — and, eventually, the merits — will shape not only Colorado's law, but the playbook for challenging every similar protection in every other state.

And the timing is not an accident. Just two weeks ago, Colorado voters approved two new ballot measures — Proposition 134, restricting trans youth in school sports, and Proposition 135, banning gender-affirming surgery for minors — which take effect January 1, 2027. We wrote about those on November 7. So Colorado is now a state where voters have just approved new restrictions on trans people and where an existing protection for trans people is being fought over in federal court, in the same month. The protection and the restriction are moving in opposite directions at the same time, and the outcome of this appeal will help decide which direction holds.

Why this matters from here

Loves, it is day five of Transgender Awareness Week, and this is what the week is actually about. Not just visibility in the abstract — but the very specific, very ordinary dignity of being called by your name when you walk into a room. Kelly Loving was murdered for being who she was, in a place that was supposed to be safe. A law bearing her name says that the rest of us — the shops, the counters, the waiting rooms, the everyday public square — will not do a smaller version of the same erasure. That is what the challengers are asking a court to strike down, and that is what yesterday's hearing was really about, underneath the standing arguments and the procedural questions.

Here is the honest place to land: the law is still in effect today. Nothing was decided yesterday except that the judges are thinking about it. The businesses did not win a block, and the state did not win a dismissal. The panel took the case under consideration, and we will be watching for its decision — and for the Colorado Supreme Court's answer on the scope of the law, which is running on a parallel track.

But there is something worth holding onto in the meantime. The fact that this fight is happening at all — that the state of Colorado passed this law, named it for a trans woman, and is defending it in federal court — is itself a measure of how far we have come. Ten years ago, there was no law like this to defend. And the fact that the challengers' own case, so far, has turned on whether they can even show they are about to be punished, tells you something about how the law actually works in practice: it is not a speech police. It is a line that says a trans person is a full member of the public, entitled to the same service as anyone else, addressed as themselves.

That line is worth defending. And on day five of Transgender Awareness Week, with Transgender Day of Remembrance three days away, it is worth saying Kelly Loving's name — and remembering what her name was put on this law to protect.

What you can do next: If you live in Colorado, the Kelly Loving Act is still in effect — your chosen name and pronouns are protected in public accommodations while this case continues. If a business refuses you service or deliberately misgenders you, you can file a complaint with the Colorado Civil Rights Division. And wherever you live, share this story: most people have never heard of this law, or of the woman it is named for, and knowing her name is part of how we keep her memory — and the protection she inspired — alive.
