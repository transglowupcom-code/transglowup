The Supreme Court Just Sent Darlene Griffith's Jail Case Back — What It Means for Trans People Behind Bars

On Monday, November 9, 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court sent a transgender woman's civil rights case against a Colorado county jail back to the appeals court for a second look — and told the lower court to reconsider it in light of the justices' two recent rulings on trans rights. Darlene Griffith's case is not over. But the legal ground underneath it just shifted, and the shift matters for every trans person in a jail or prison cell in America.

November 9, 2026 · 6 min read

Who is Darlene Griffith, and what happened to her?

Loves, let's start with the person at the center of this, because court cases have a way of turning people into footnotes, and Darlene Griffith is not a footnote. She is a transgender woman who has been living openly as a woman for more than twenty years. She has been diagnosed with gender dysphoria, and as part of her medically supervised treatment she changed her name, takes feminizing hormones, and lives in every way as the woman she is.

In the summer of 2020, Griffith entered the El Paso County Jail in Colorado as a pretrial detainee — someone who had not been convicted of anything and was waiting for her case to be heard. She asked to be housed in a female unit. She told the jail she was a transgender woman, that her medical records confirmed her gender dysphoria, and that she feared being constantly searched by male guards and being treated as a man. The jail refused. Its policy, as the appeals court later described it, was to house transgender women in male units, and to have them searched — including strip searched — by male staff.

What Griffith says happened next is the part that is hard to read and harder to live through. She alleges she was repeatedly sexually assaulted by a male inmate, frequently searched by male deputies, subjected to a visual body-cavity search by male staff during intake, misgendered by staff, and denied access to commissary items like women's underwear and lipstick. She says these conditions worsened her gender dysphoria and caused her extreme emotional distress. She lived under them for more than a year before the jail finally moved her to a women's unit.

While she was still inside, Griffith filed a civil rights lawsuit — a sixteen-count complaint against the county, the sheriff, and individual deputies, bringing claims under the Constitution, the Americans with Disabilities Act, and the Rehabilitation Act. A federal district court dismissed the whole thing. She appealed, and that is where the case started its long climb.

The case so far: a partial win, then a trip to the Supreme Court

On February 19, 2025, the Denver-based Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals gave Griffith a partial victory. The court said several of her claims could go forward: her Fourteenth Amendment equal protection claim against the sheriff over the jail's sex-based housing policy, her Fourth and Fourteenth Amendment claims about cross-gender searches, and a Fourth Amendment claim about an abusive strip search by a deputy. The court also vacated the dismissal of her disability claims. It was not a ruling that the jail had violated her rights — the case was still at the motion-to-dismiss stage, where a court only asks whether the claims are strong enough to proceed. But it meant her case would get a real hearing instead of being thrown out at the door.

The sheriff's office asked the full Tenth Circuit to rehear the case. On June 10, 2025, that request was denied. So the county appealed to the Supreme Court, arguing that prison administrators were being "subject to inconsistent mandates on how to classify and house transgender inmates while maintaining safety and security in their facilities." The sheriff is now Joseph J. Roybal, which is why the Supreme Court case is called Roybal v. Griffith, docket number 25-442.

The Justice Department weighed in, too — and its position is important to understand what happened on Monday. The department told the justices that the Tenth Circuit's 2025 ruling came before the Supreme Court's two recent decisions on transgender issues, and that the appeals court should be told to take another look. In those two rulings, the Court upheld a state ban on gender-affirming care for minors and upheld bans on transgender athletes competing on female teams. Under that "clear guidance," the Justice Department argued, the jail's housing policy cannot be challenged. "Like sports and medicine," the department's lawyers wrote, "prisons are another area where 'everyone agrees' that some sex-based classifications are permitted."

What the Supreme Court actually did on Monday

Here is the part to get exactly right, because it is easy to misread. On November 9, the Supreme Court did not rule that the jail's policy is constitutional. It did not rule that Griffith's claims fail. And it did not decide the case on the merits at all.

What the Court did is called a GVR: it granted the county's petition, vacated the Tenth Circuit's judgment, and remanded the case — sent it back — for further consideration in light of the Court's rulings in United States v. Skrmetti and West Virginia v. B.P.J. A GVR is a short, unsigned order. It is the Court's way of saying: the law has changed since the lower court ruled, so go look at this again under the new rules. It is not a loss for Griffith in the way a ruling against her would be. But it is not a win, either. It is a reset — and the new rules the Tenth Circuit has to apply are less favorable to her than the ones it applied the first time.

The reason is the standard of review, which is the dry-sounding legal question at the heart of the whole case. When a court looks at a government policy that treats people differently based on sex, it has to decide how hard to look. Under United States v. Virginia, the 1996 case about the Virginia Military Institute, sex-based classifications get heightened scrutiny — the government has to show a strong justification. But under Turner v. Safley, a 1987 case about prison rules, courts give prison administrators wide latitude: a prison regulation is valid if it is reasonably related to a legitimate penological interest. The Tenth Circuit had wrestled with that tension and let Griffith's equal protection claim proceed. Then came Skrmetti, in June 2025, in which the Supreme Court held that laws classifying people based on transgender status are subject only to rational-basis review — the easiest standard for the government to meet. That is the ruling the Tenth Circuit now has to reckon with.

So the question going back to the appeals court is, roughly: does a jail's policy of housing trans women with men still deserve a hard look, or does the government's easiest justification now suffice? How the Tenth Circuit answers that will decide whether Griffith's claims survive — and it will set a precedent that other courts in the Tenth Circuit's six-state region will follow when trans people in custody bring their own cases.

Why this reaches far beyond one jail in Colorado

Griffith's case is one piece of a much bigger fight over how trans people are treated behind bars, and that fight is moving on several fronts at once. In January 2025, President Trump directed the federal Bureau of Prisons to house inmates based on their biological sex — an order that incarcerated transgender women are challenging in court. The Bureau of Prisons then adopted a policy ending hormone therapy and other transition-related care for trans people in federal custody. Three transgender inmates sued, a federal judge blocked the policy in August 2026, and on October 21, 2026, the Supreme Court declined to let the administration enforce the policy while the lawsuit plays out. That was a real, if temporary, protection: the justices refused the emergency request, and hormone therapy and related care must continue for now. Justices Thomas and Alito said they would have granted the request.

Put the two October and November orders side by side and the picture gets complicated in a way that is worth sitting with honestly. In one case, the Court kept care in place for federal inmates while the challenge proceeds. In the other, it sent a trans woman's housing and search claims back to be re-judged under rulings that make those claims harder to win. Neither order decides the underlying question — whether the Constitution protects trans people in custody from being housed and searched according to their sex assigned at birth. But both show how much of that question now turns on Skrmetti and B.P.J., and on how lower courts read them.

That is the part that should concern anyone who cares about trans health, even if you have never set foot in a jail. The same legal reasoning that decides whether a jail can house a trans woman with men is the reasoning that will be cited in the next case about Medicaid coverage, the next case about school sports, and the next case about a doctor's ability to provide care. Courts borrow each other's logic. A ruling about housing becomes a paragraph in a ruling about medicine. Griffith's case matters because it is where the logic of the Court's recent trans rulings meets a person in a cell — a person who says she was assaulted, searched, and misgendered because of a policy that sorted her by her sex assigned at birth.

What to watch next

The case now goes back to the Tenth Circuit, which covers Colorado, Kansas, New Mexico, Oklahoma, Utah, and Wyoming. There is no set timetable for the appeals court to rule again, and its new decision could itself be appealed back to the Supreme Court. Meanwhile, Griffith's underlying claims — the equal protection challenge, the cross-gender search claims, the abusive search claim — are still alive. They have not been dismissed. They are waiting for the appeals court to apply the new law and say whether they can proceed.

What we will be watching, loves, is whether the Tenth Circuit finds a way to keep the claims alive under Skrmetti's rational-basis framework — for example, by finding that even under the easier standard, the jail's policy as applied to Griffith fails — or whether it concludes that the new rulings close the door. We will also be watching the federal prison cases, where the October 21 order keeping hormone therapy in place is only a pause, not a final answer. And we will keep saying the plain thing that the legal arguments keep circling around: the people at the center of these cases are real, they are in cells right now, and the policies being debated decide whether they are safe.

If you or someone you love is trans and in custody, or has a loved one who is, know that you are not forgotten in this coverage. The courtroom language is dry by design. The stakes are not. Darlene Griffith waited more than a year in a men's unit for a transfer to a women's unit. She filed her case from inside the jail. Whatever the Tenth Circuit decides on the second look, her story is now part of the record the courts will have to answer — and we will be here, telling you what happens next, in plain words.
