One Week to Dec. 3: Washington's Girls Are Scrambling for “Biological Sex” Exams — and Seattle Just Asked a Judge to Stop It

One week from today, every girl who wants to play school sports in Washington will need a doctor's note proving her “biological sex” — or she sits out. Families are flooding doctors' offices to get the forms signed, clinics are opening pop-up attestation visits, and the state's biggest city, its biggest county, and its biggest school district just went to court together to ask a judge to stop the whole thing before it starts.

November 26, 2026 · 8 min read

Seven days, one form, 118,000 girls

Loves, let's start with the numbers, because they tell the whole story in one breath. Washington voters approved Initiative 638 on November 3 with about 56 percent of the vote. The measure bars trans girls from girls' school sports — and it requires every girl on a girls' team to have a health care provider sign a form verifying her “biological sex.” The law takes effect December 3.

Here is who that actually touches. According to the Washington Interscholastic Activities Association, about ten transgender student-athletes compete statewide each year — the association itself says it does not keep precise statistics. The same association counts about 118,000 girls participating in high school athletics every school year. So a law written to exclude roughly ten kids now requires a doctor's signature from more than a hundred thousand girls, in the middle of a winter season that is already underway.

That is the third time we have written about this law in two weeks — we covered the lawsuit when it was filed on November 9, and the attorney general's outside defense team on November 15 — because the story keeps changing, and every change has been a new problem the law's supporters said would not happen. This week's change is the most concrete one yet: the paperwork is here, the deadline is real, and families are running out of time.

The form itself is one page, released by the WIAA last week. A health care provider checks “male” or “female” based on their “professional medical assessment and our records,” and certifies that they have reviewed the medical information or findings needed to make that determination. The law says the verification may rest on one or more of three things: reproductive anatomy, genetic makeup, or the testosterone levels a body normally produces. Boys on boys' teams need no such paperwork. Girls — and only girls — do.

Washington has allowed trans students to play on teams that match their gender identity since 2007, when the WIAA became the first state association in the country to adopt such a policy. Initiative 638 would end nearly two decades of that. And it does it by putting a medical exam between every girl and her sport.

The scramble

Erin Reed, writing in Erin in the Morning on November 23, called it what it is: chaos. Doctors' offices are reporting mass confusion and being flooded with requests to sign the forms from parents who suddenly realized they may need to take their daughters into the exam room just to keep playing. KXLY's Melissa Luck, reporting from Spokane, put it plainly on social media: “The consensus: chaos. Doctors offices are scrambling to determine procedures and families aren't sure what they need.” She added the detail that makes the timing cruel — the winter sports season had already started, and players need a certain number of practices before they can play in games.

Dr. Jennifer Kalisvaart, a pediatrician in Spokane, told KXLY her office had received more than 20 of the forms since the previous Thursday. “I didn't realize what a quick turnaround they would need for the forms to be completed,” she said.

Even parents who supported the law are finding themselves scrambling. Chelsea McKell, a parent who backs the measure, told KXLY that getting her daughters' forms signed has already been a nightmare. “Moms are busy. We have a lot to do, and that form is one more thing on our to-do list,” she said — though she added that she still supports the law.

And today is Thanksgiving. Many doctors' offices are closed for the holiday, in the middle of the one week families have left. That is the calendar the law's supporters built: a deadline of December 3, with a holiday week in between, for more than a hundred thousand forms.

The scramble has already created its own little economy. In Poulsbo, a group of local medical professionals — including parents of girls in sports — opened pop-up attestation visits at the Atlas Wellness Center this week: $50 a visit, $30 per sibling, sliding scale available, with blood-test options for testosterone or chromosomes instead of a physical exam. The clinic's own description is careful and respectful — a gown, underwear and bra on throughout, no pelvic exam, a staff chaperone present, a parent allowed in the room — and it is also a sign of how far from normal this is: a pop-up clinic, charging families by the visit, so their daughters can keep playing basketball.

Some districts are trying to soften the blow. Lake Washington School District told families it will credit $20 to the accounts of students competing in a girls' sport who turn in a completed form this season, recognizing the cost families may incur to get a provider to sign. Puyallup School District posted guidance on November 23 laying out two options: have a provider complete the WIAA attestation form, or get a new sports physical on the updated WIAA form that includes the verification. Either way, the district says, the documentation has to be on file by December 3.

But money and guidance only go so far when there is no doctor to see. About a quarter of children in Washington — roughly 413,000 kids — do not have a personal primary care physician, according to the medical groups opposing the law. Genetic testing or testosterone testing can be costly and is typically not covered by insurance, hitting low-income families hardest. And families were alerted only about ten business days before the deadline, while getting a doctor's appointment can take weeks, if not months.

The Washington School Based Health Alliance has suggested providers “strongly consider scheduling any anticipated sports physical appointments as close to December 3 as possible,” to minimize unnecessary appointments and allow cancellations if a judge pauses the law. Read that again: the state's own school health experts are telling doctors to schedule girls' appointments for the last possible minute, because nobody knows whether the law will even be in effect by then.

The doctors said this would happen

None of this should surprise the law's supporters, because the doctors warned them — on the record, weeks ago. On November 17, seven medical groups, led by the Washington State Medical Association and including the Washington chapter of the American Academy of Pediatrics, issued a statement supporting the lawsuit against the law and asking for its implementation to be suspended. Their core complaint was that the initiative's terms lack “clinical specificity” and put physicians in an “untenable position.”

Their concerns, in plain language: the law does not define what counts as a “health examination,” and it does not say whether doctors must perform genital examinations. Routine sports physicals never include genital examinations for girls — those are only performed when medically necessary. The vague term “reproductive anatomy” could be read as requiring external or internal examinations, which could cause significant anxiety and fear among young patients. Some girls, the doctors warned, may simply give up playing sports altogether.

“Genital exams are not part of today's routine sports physicals and are not medically necessary,” the groups wrote. Dr. Glenna Martin, a family physician at a Seattle community health center, told the Seattle Times in October: “This is so clearly written by somebody who does not understand what I'm doing in a sports physical.” Of the tests the law contemplates, she said, “At the end of the day, I wouldn't do it because I think it is completely unethical.”

Put the two halves together and you see the trap the law sets for doctors. The form asks a provider to put their license behind a determination of a child's sex. If they follow the law's text, they may need to perform a genital exam, draw blood for a testosterone test, or run a genetic test such as a cheek swab. If they refuse on ethical grounds, a girl loses her season. The doctors did not create this dilemma. The voters were told it would not exist. It does.

Seattle, King County and the schools go to court

That is the backdrop for what happened in court this week. On Tuesday, November 24, the City of Seattle and King County filed a joint amicus brief — a friend-of-the-court filing — asking King County Superior Court Judge Maureen McKee to block Initiative 638 before it takes effect. Mayor Katie Wilson, County Executive Girmay Zahilay and City Attorney Erika Evans announced the filing. Seattle Public Schools filed its own brief the week before, on November 18, the same night the plaintiffs asked for a preliminary injunction.

The school district's brief is the most practical document in the case, because it comes from the people who would actually have to enforce the law. The district told the court it agrees with the plaintiffs that the measure severely infringes on the Washington Constitution's guaranteed right to privacy and its Equal Rights Amendment, and that it does not want to implement a measure it believes is unconstitutional. Then it gave the court a number: about 2,000 students are on girls' winter sports rosters in Seattle Public Schools this school year, and about 1,400 of them have a current physical on file that contains no verification of sex and will not expire in the next 90 days. Absent an order from Judge McKee, the district concluded, those girls would have to be benched if the law goes into effect next week.

“The State has a real interest in giving effect to a measure the voters approved, but I-638's unclear mandates, coupled with a fast-approaching December 3, 2026 effective date, have made it difficult for the District to implement the Initiative as intended,” the district's lawyers wrote. “An order pausing section 3(2) of the Initiative pauses paperwork: no student gains or loses a place on a team because a verification statement goes uncollected this winter.”

Seattle and King County's joint brief makes a different argument, and it is a strong one: the initiative conflicts with their own nondiscrimination laws. King County voters added gender identity or expression to the county charter's anti-discrimination protections back in 2008, and the brief points out that schools using county fields and facilities are obliged to comply with local nondiscrimination provisions as a condition of their use agreements. “Preventing transgender girls from competing in girls' sporting events and requiring only girls, not boys, to provide medical verification of their biological sex is per se discrimination and is illegal in King County,” the brief says. It also warns that the county's ban on contracting with entities that discriminate could end up denying all young athletes — boys, girls, trans athletes or otherwise — access to King County athletic facilities.

The briefs also remind the court who voted how: more than three quarters of Seattle voters rejected Initiative 638, and close to three fifths of King County voters opposed it. The law passed statewide by about 12 points. In the county where it will be litigated, it lost.

The lawsuit itself was filed November 9 by TransFamilies, the Washington Education Association and parents of student athletes, represented by the ACLU of Washington, Legal Voice and Pacifica Law Group. It argues the law violates the Washington Constitution on multiple grounds, including the privacy right in Article I, Section 7, the Equal Rights Amendment, and Article IX's guarantee of education without distinction on account of sex. The motion for a preliminary injunction was filed November 18. Judge McKee will hear it at 9 a.m. on December 3 — the very morning the law takes effect. District leaders in Jefferson County told families they do not expect a decision before then, which means schools and families are planning as if the law will go into force on schedule.

Attorney General Nick Brown's office, which declined to defend the law itself, has hired the international firm Bryan Cave Leighton Paisner to argue the state's side. The ban's supporters, through the Alliance Defending Freedom, have separately asked to join the case as defendants, arguing the state cannot adequately represent their interests.

Meanwhile, fourteen attorneys general weigh in

Washington is not the only place the sports fight moved this week. On November 25, Maryland Attorney General Anthony G. Brown joined a coalition of fourteen attorneys general in filing an amicus brief with the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, urging the court to uphold the right of trans student athletes to compete on teams that match their gender identity. The brief defends Washington's existing inclusive policies — the ones Initiative 638 would replace — and argues that federal law does not require schools to exclude trans athletes.

The case is a different one from the state-court challenge to 638. In June, Stephanie Lynn Brown and her daughter K.M.K., a cisgender female wrestler, sued the Washington Interscholastic Activities Association under Title IX, challenging the policies that let students compete on the team matching their gender identity. A federal district court denied their request for a preliminary injunction in August, and they appealed. The attorneys general — from California, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, the District of Columbia, Illinois, Maine, Massachusetts, Minnesota, Nevada, New York, Oregon and Vermont, alongside Maryland — are asking the Ninth Circuit to uphold that denial.

Their brief leans on the Supreme Court's ruling in West Virginia v. B.P.J., which held that schools may determine athletic eligibility based on sex assigned at birth, but are not required to under Title IX — leaving the choice to schools and legislatures. It also cites the U.S. Transgender Survey, the largest survey of trans people in the country, which found that more than three quarters of respondents reported mistreatment or negative experiences at school, more than half reported verbal harassment, almost a quarter reported a physical attack, about one in eight reported being sexually assaulted, and more than one in six left school because of the mistreatment. Discrimination in school, the brief argues, harms trans students' physical and mental health and their education — while inclusive policies improve outcomes.

Two court fights, two different courts, one week apart: in state court, Washington's biggest governments are asking a judge to pause a ban before it benches 1,400 girls in one district alone. In federal court, fourteen states are defending the inclusive rules that ban would erase. Both cases turn, in the end, on the same question: who gets to decide what a girl has to prove to play?

What this means for trans health

Let's be honest about what happens if Judge McKee does not pause the law on December 3. Trans girls in Washington will be barred from girls' teams — that was always the point of the measure. But the damage will not stop there. About 1,400 girls in Seattle alone, and tens of thousands statewide, will be benched until a doctor signs a form — girls whose only offense is that their perfectly valid sports physical, done months ago, lacks a box that did not exist when it was signed. Some will get the form signed. Some will not get an appointment in time. Some, the doctors warn, will look at what the form might require and simply quit.

That is the part of this story that matters most for trans health, and it is the part the law's supporters never campaigned on. Sports are health. For teenagers, they are exercise, friendship, belonging, and one of the few places a kid who feels different gets to feel like part of a team. The U.S. Transgender Survey numbers in the attorneys general brief are not abstract: trans students who face mistreatment at school report worse physical and mental health, and inclusive policies improve both. A law that pushes trans girls out of sports, and makes every other girl prove herself to stay in, is a health policy — whether or not anyone calls it one.

There are honest limits to the hope here, too. A preliminary injunction is not a final ruling. If Judge McKee pauses the law, the case continues, and the state will appeal. If she does not, the law goes into effect on December 3 and stays in effect while the case is litigated, with girls benched in the meantime. Either way, the fight is months from over. And even a win in King County Superior Court would not touch the similar measures voters approved this month in Arizona, Colorado and Nebraska — or the ones the far right has already announced it plans to put on ballots in more states.

But this week also showed something the law's supporters did not plan for: the people who have to live with the law are the ones fighting it hardest. Not just trans families, but school districts, city governments, county governments, and seven medical groups. The measure was sold as a simple rule about fairness. A week before it takes effect, it looks like what the doctors said it was — a vague, intrusive, unworkable mandate that punishes the very girls it claimed to protect.

December 3 is one week away. The hearing is at 9 a.m. that morning. We will be watching — and we will tell you what the judge decides.

What you can do next: If you are a Washington family, check your school district's guidance now — Puyallup, Lake Washington and other districts have posted their requirements and deadlines, and the WIAA attestation form is available through your school. If your daughter's physical is current, ask your provider to complete the attestation form rather than booking a whole new physical. And keep an eye on the December 3 hearing before Judge McKee in King County Superior Court: if she grants the injunction, the current rules stay in place while the case is decided.
