One Month In: The Medicaid Rule Cutting Off Care for 130,000 Trans Youth — and the Clock That's Now Running

A federal rule that took effect on October 13, 2026, now bars Medicaid and CHIP funds from paying for gender-affirming care for trans young people. About 130,000 trans youth on those programs live in states where the care was still legal — and a six-month countdown is now running for the kids who were already on hormone therapy.

November 8, 2026 · 6 min read

What the rule actually does

Loves, let's start with the plain version, because the official language is designed to confuse you. On August 11, 2026, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services — CMS, the federal agency that runs Medicaid — issued a final rule, published in the Federal Register on August 13, that prohibits federal Medicaid dollars from covering puberty blockers, hormone therapy, and surgery for enrollees under 18 when the care is for gender transition. The same restriction applies to the Children's Health Insurance Program, CHIP, for enrollees under 19. The rule took effect on October 13, 2026.

CMS calls these treatments "sex-rejecting procedures." That is the agency's term, not a medical one. The care itself has not changed: puberty blockers pause puberty, hormone therapy brings a young person's body in line with who they are, and surgery for minors is, in the words of the health-policy organization KFF, "very rarely used among young people." What changed is who pays for it. The rule cuts off the federal money — it does not make the care itself illegal, and it does not stop a state from paying for the same care with state-only dollars.

There are two carve-outs worth knowing. First, the rule does not touch counseling or psychotherapy provided as part of gender-affirming care — that coverage continues. Second, the same medications are still covered when they are prescribed for other reasons. Puberty blockers for a cisgender child with precocious puberty, for example, and surgeries for intersex infants are excluded from the rule's definition entirely. The funding disappears only when the patient is trans. That detail matters, and it is one of the reasons the rule is being challenged in court.

Who it hits

About half of the states already restrict access to gender-affirming care for minors through their own laws — a number that has grown from just four states in 2023 to 27 today, according to KFF. The federal rule reaches past those states into the ones that did not ban the care themselves. KFF estimates there are about 130,000 young trans people with Medicaid or CHIP coverage who live in states without restrictions. Those are the kids who, until October 13, could get this care covered — and who now face new limits unless their state steps in with its own money.

And these are, by definition, low-income families. Medicaid and CHIP exist for people who cannot afford private insurance, and roughly 37 percent of all people under 18 in the United States are covered by one of the two programs. When the federal money stops, the realistic options are: the state pays, a charity pays, the family pays out of pocket, or the care stops. For most families on Medicaid, paying cash is not a real option. KFF puts it plainly: given the income limits in the Medicaid program, "it is not likely many would have the resources to do so."

The Youth Law Center flagged a group that is especially exposed in its November 2026 federal youth policy briefing: young people in foster care. Medicaid has a longstanding guarantee — the Early and Periodic Screening, Diagnostic, and Treatment benefit — that generally requires states to cover medically necessary services for eligible young people under 21. Foster youth do not have a parent who can shop for another plan or pay a bill out of pocket. When the federal rule carves this care out of Medicaid, the young people with the fewest alternatives feel it first.

The six-month clock

Here is the part with a deadline on it. When CMS proposed this rule in December 2025, there was no transition period at all. The agency received nearly 35,000 comments on the proposal, and of the roughly 11,000 comments that were posted, CMS itself writes that more than 90 percent opposed the policy change. In the final rule, CMS added one concession: for young people who were already receiving hormone therapy when the rule took effect, federal funds can keep covering that therapy "for a tapering period of up to 6 months." CMS said it added the provision "to provide beneficiaries and their treating providers a reasonable opportunity to phase off these medications in a manner that allows for clinical discretion, if desired."

That clock started on October 13, which means it runs out in roughly mid-April 2027. And it is narrower than it sounds. The tapering period applies only to hormone therapy — not to puberty blockers, and not to surgery. For puberty blockers, federal funding ended on the effective date. And it applies only to young people who were already on hormones as of October 13. A young person who had not yet started treatment has no tapering period at all: the federal money was gone from day one.

So the real question for the next five months is what the states do. Some already have. New York Governor Kathy Hochul has committed to using state Medicaid dollars so young people there can keep getting the care they need, and New Mexico Governor Michelle Lujan Grisham has committed state dollars to cover gender-affirming medications. Other states may follow — or they may not. In states that do nothing, the tapering period is not a bridge to continued care. It is a countdown.

The money, in perspective

It is worth sitting with the numbers, because they tell you what this rule is and is not about. CMS estimates that Medicaid spent about $31 million on the prohibited services for enrollees under 19 in 2023. That sounds like a lot of money until you put it next to the program it comes from: it represents 0.003 percent of all Medicaid spending that year. KFF's assessment is blunt — absorbing these costs with state funds "would likely be a relatively small expenditure increase for states," given how relatively uncommon and inexpensive the services are.

Meanwhile, the medical consensus has not moved. Gender-affirming care remains a best medical practice recommended by major medical groups, including the American Medical Association, the American Academy of Pediatrics, and the American Psychological Association. CMS's own rule leans instead on an internal federal health report and on international reviews that question the evidence base — the same framing the administration has used across a series of actions restricting this care. The disagreement is not new. What is new is that the disagreement is now being settled at the payer level, where it lands directly on the families with the least money to absorb it.

The fight in court

The rule is being challenged. On September 2, 2026, a coalition of 21 state attorneys general and Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro sued to stop it, filing in the U.S. District Court for the District of Massachusetts. The coalition is co-led by the attorneys general of California, Illinois, Maryland, Connecticut, and Massachusetts, and it argues that CMS exceeded its authority by imposing a nationwide ban on federal reimbursement for care that states had chosen to cover. The states are asking the court to declare the rule unlawful and block the administration from implementing or enforcing it.

The rule took effect on October 13 while that challenge was pending, and as of a mid-October review of court trackers, no order blocking the rule had been identified. That is the honest status: the lawsuit is live, the rule is live, and the courts have not yet stopped it. Families in New Hampshire have also brought their own challenge on behalf of trans adolescents who rely on Medicaid. The legal fight will take months, and the tapering clock will keep running while it does.

There is one more piece of context that helps explain why this rule landed the way it did. The December 2025 proposal was one of two rules CMS put forward that month. The other — a proposed change to hospital conditions of participation — would have gone further, barring most hospitals that accept Medicare or Medicaid from providing this care to young people at all, regardless of who pays. That hospital rule is still only a proposal. The Medicaid funding rule is the one that became final, and it is the one now in effect.

What this means, honestly

So where does that leave us, one month in? If your family is on Medicaid or CHIP in a state that already banned this care, the federal rule changes little on the ground — the state ban was already the barrier. If you are in a state that still allows the care, everything now depends on what your state does with its own money. New York and New Mexico have committed state funds. Other states have not yet said. And if you are a young person who was already on hormones on October 13, the tapering period gives you and your provider time to plan — but it is time with an end date, and the plan it buys has to account for a world where the federal money does not come back.

The pattern is worth naming plainly, because it is the same pattern we have been writing about all week. The election decided a lot of things about trans health at the ballot box. But some of the biggest changes never appear on a ballot at all. A rule published in the Federal Register in August, written in bureaucratic language, quietly rerouted the money for 130,000 young people's care — and most families only found out when the coverage stopped. That is how access gets taken away without a vote: not with a ban, but with a funding line.

None of that means the fight is over. The lawsuit from 21 states is real, the states that have stepped in with their own money are real, and the tapering period is real time — time to organize, to pressure state lawmakers, to find other coverage, and to make sure the families affected know what is happening before the money runs out. But it does mean the work is urgent in a way that a headline about an election is not. Elections open doors. Funding rules close them quietly, one claim at a time.

If you are reading this and you are a young person on Medicaid or CHIP, or the parent of one, here is what matters most right now: your coverage for counseling and psychotherapy has not changed. Your state may still cover the medical care with its own funds — check with your provider and your state Medicaid office, not with a rumor. And if you were already on hormones on October 13, talk to your provider now about the tapering period and what comes after it. You deserve a plan, not a surprise. And you deserve care from people who see you clearly — which is exactly why we will keep writing about this until the clock stops running.

What you can do next: If your family is on Medicaid or CHIP, ask your provider or your state Medicaid office whether your state is covering gender-affirming care with state-only funds — New York and New Mexico already have. If you were on hormones as of October 13, ask your provider about the six-month tapering period and plan ahead for mid-April 2027.
