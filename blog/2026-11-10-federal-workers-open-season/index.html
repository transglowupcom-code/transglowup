Open Season, Closed Doors: Federal Workers Are Asking a Judge to Save Their Health Coverage

Federal Benefits Open Season opened yesterday, November 9, 2026 — the five-week window when millions of federal workers and retirees pick their health insurance for next year. But for trans federal workers and their families, every plan on the table comes with the same catch: the gender-affirming care they need is not covered. Five of those workers are now asking a federal judge to step in before enrollment closes on December 14.

November 10, 2026 · 5 min read

How the coverage disappeared

Loves, this did not happen overnight — it happened in a chain of memos, and the dates matter, because they show how fast a benefit people had relied on for years was taken away.

On January 20, 2025, the day of his inauguration, President Donald Trump signed Executive Order 14168, titled "Defending Women from Gender Ideology Extremism and Restoring Biological Truth to the Federal Government." Acting on that order, the U.S. Office of Personnel Management — the agency that runs health insurance for federal workers — issued a carrier letter on August 15, 2025, telling every insurer in the Federal Employees Health Benefits (FEHB) and Postal Service Health Benefits (PSHB) programs that, starting January 1, 2026, coverage for "chemical and surgical modification of an individual's sex traits through medical interventions" — in other words, gender-affirming care — would no longer be covered.

There was one narrow lifeline in that first letter. For 2026 only, insurers could run an exceptions process for people who were already mid-treatment — already on hormone therapy, or already in a surgical care plan for diagnosed gender dysphoria. Counseling stayed covered. But on March 31, 2026, OPM issued a second carrier letter telling insurers to remove that exceptions process entirely for plan year 2027. So the people who were mid-treatment in 2026 — the ones the first letter said would be protected — are the very people who lose that protection on January 1.

That is the situation trans federal workers are staring at as they pick their 2027 plans this week: the care their doctors prescribed is excluded from every plan, and the exception that covered them this year is gone next year.

Five workers sued — and now they are asking for emergency help

On August 3, 2026, five federal employees filed a class-action lawsuit against OPM Director Scott Kupor in the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia. The case is Doe et al. v. Kupor, Civil Action No. 26-2723. The plaintiffs — transgender and gender-nonconforming federal employees, plus one employee with a transgender dependent — are suing under pseudonyms, and a court order granted that request in part. They argue that stripping gender-affirming care from federal employee health plans is sex discrimination, prohibited by Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964.

Their argument is straightforward, and it is worth stating plainly: the same hormones and surgeries that are covered when a cisgender employee needs them are denied when a trans employee needs them for gender dysphoria. The only difference is the sex of the person asking. That, the lawsuit says, is discrimination on the basis of sex — and federal employment law has prohibited that since 1964.

On November 4, 2026, the plaintiffs' lawyers — the Human Rights Campaign Foundation and the firms Correia & Puth and Cohen Milstein Sellers & Toll — asked the court for a preliminary injunction: an emergency order that would block OPM from enforcing the coverage ban while the case continues. They timed the request to the enrollment calendar on purpose. Open Season runs November 9 through December 14, and once workers lock in their 2027 plans, the new exclusions take effect — January 1, 2027, for postal workers and January 10, 2027, for most other federal employees. A ruling after those dates would come too late for the year ahead.

As of this writing, no ruling has been issued on that request. In a joint filing on November 6, the two sides proposed competing schedules for briefing the motion, and the court had not yet set one. The government opposes the injunction. So for now, the workers are choosing their 2027 coverage with the ban still in place — and the clock is running.

How many people this touches

The scale here is easy to miss, because the workers at the center of the case are suing under pseudonyms and the numbers come from a research institute, not a press conference. A new study from the Williams Institute at UCLA estimates that at least 39,400 transgender people are enrolled in the FEHB and PSHB programs — current and former federal employees and their dependents. Some estimates put the number higher, up to about 57,800. That is tens of thousands of people whose access to care now depends on a court case most of them have never heard of.

And the harm is not abstract. The Human Rights Campaign Foundation has said the coverage ban has already forced federal employees to leave their jobs, take on heavier financial burdens, or go without care altogether. Think about what that means in real life: a postal worker who has been on hormone therapy for years suddenly paying for it out of pocket, or stopping it. A parent whose teenager's care was covered last year now choosing between a bill they cannot afford and a child going without treatment their doctor prescribed. A federal employee at the Department of Health and Human Services — one of the plaintiffs works there — whose own employer's insurance stopped covering the care that keeps them healthy.

It is also worth naming who is not in this story: no one is asking for special treatment. These are employees with the same insurance everyone else in the federal workforce gets. The lawsuit asks for the same coverage rules to apply to them as to their coworkers.

Why this fight matters beyond the federal workforce

If you are not a federal worker, you might wonder why this one matters to you. Here is why, loves: the federal employee health program is the largest employer-sponsored health insurance program in the country, and it sets a pattern. When the federal government writes a coverage exclusion into its own plans, other employers and insurers watch — and some follow.

It is also the same playbook we have been tracking all year. Just yesterday, we wrote about the federal Medicaid rule that took effect October 13 and cut off funding for gender-affirming care for trans youth, with a six-month taper now running for kids already on hormones. The federal employee ban is the other half of that picture: one rule takes coverage from trans kids on Medicaid, another takes it from trans adults and their families who work for the government. Same year, same strategy, different populations. And both are being fought in court — the Medicaid rule by 21 state attorneys general, this one by the workers themselves.

There is also a practical lesson in the timing. Open Season is a deadline, and deadlines are where policy becomes personal. Most people pick their health plan in a rush, between work and everything else, and assume the coverage will be there when they need it. For trans federal workers this year, that assumption is wrong — and the only fix on the table right now is a judge's order.

What you can do next: If you are a federal employee, retiree, or postal worker — or you love someone who is — Open Season runs through Monday, December 14, 2026. Read your plan's 2027 brochure carefully before you enroll, because the exclusions are in the fine print, and your current coverage generally rolls over automatically if you make no changes. If you are affected by this case, the Human Rights Campaign is posting updates at hrc.org/fehb-class-action.
