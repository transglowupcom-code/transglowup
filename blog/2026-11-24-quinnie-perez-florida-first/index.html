Florida Just Elected Its First Openly Trans Official — a Farmworker Who Ran on Clean Water and Showing Up

Three weeks after election night, the story is still sinking in: Quinnie Pérez, a 32-year-old medical cannabis farmworker and the daughter of Mexican farmworkers, won a seat on Orange County's Soil and Water Conservation District on November 3, 2026 — becoming the first openly transgender person ever elected to public office in Florida. She did it in the state that has spent years trying to legislate trans people out of public life. She did it by knocking on doors.

November 24, 2026 · 7 min read

The win, in plain language

Loves, here is what happened. On Tuesday, November 3, 2026, Orange County voters elected Quinnie Pérez to Seat 1 on the county's Soil and Water Conservation District Board of Supervisors. She took nearly 37 percent of the more than 96,000 votes cast — about 35,500 votes — finishing ahead of Doug Sahr, who took about 32 percent, and Vicente Rafael Pérez Carreño, who took about 18 percent. Another 13 percent of voters left the race blank. The LGBTQ+ Victory Fund, which endorsed and supported her candidacy, confirmed she is the first openly transgender woman — and the first openly transgender person — ever elected to public office in Florida.

Seat 1 covers a broad swath of southwest Orange County, including Hunter's Creek, Winter Garden, Oakland, and Lake Buena Vista. She will take office in January 2027, joining a nonpartisan, volunteer board that helps promote conservation and the responsible management of local soil and water resources.

And yes, the job itself is small. The board has no budget, no power to tax, no paycheck, and not even an official email address — members use their personal email and volunteer for every role, from treasurer to website updates. The district's own budget plan projects about $3,000 in cash and nearly $1,000 in expenses, and its most critical budget item is a donation of space and services from Orange County government, valued at about $11,000. Lawmakers in Tallahassee have tried more than once to abolish the conservation districts outright.

That is exactly why the win matters. Nobody handed Quinnie Pérez a powerful office. She ran for the humblest seat on the ballot, in a part of the county she herself called its most conservative corner — and she won it anyway, on the merits of a campaign about farmworkers, clean water, and showing up.

A campaign that started at 4 a.m.

Pérez describes herself as the daughter of Mexican farmworkers. She grew up in Ocala, in North Florida, surrounded by agriculture and family traditions, and she now works alongside agricultural laborers in Central Florida's medical cannabis industry. That work shaped the campaign: she says she has watched immigrant workers struggle with low wages and housing insecurity, and she ran on strengthening protections for farmworkers and advocating for better pay.

Her platform was built from the ground up. Farmworker conditions, fair wages, and safety. Home rule. Urban sprawl and smart growth. Preserving Florida's wetlands and other sensitive ecosystems. Affordable, environmentally conscious housing. Development practices that protect water quality. On her campaign website, she put it plainly: "We must use all tools available to ensure that we not only have clean water for consumption and food production, but also for our environment and the wildlife that inhabit it."

The campaign itself was a grind. A typical day started at 4 a.m. so she could clock in at her day job by 6 a.m. After work, she would, in her words, "hit the pavement" — canvassing, attending events, introducing herself to voters one at a time. She raised about $6,000, a modest war chest by most political standards — but more than any other candidate in the district's races. A fellow board member-elect, Jimm Middleton, who won the district's Seat 5, put it this way: "She was out there raising money and getting a bunch of endorsements and personally greeting voters."

She did not expect to win. "I'm still in shock," she told the Winter Park Voice the morning after the election. "Last night was definitely something else. I won in probably the most conservative part of Orange County. I did not expect to win."

Being trans, on her own terms

Pérez began identifying as transgender about four years ago, while still in her 20s. She has spoken about experiencing depression and burnout before she began to better understand what made her feel healthy and "more like myself." When she considered running for office, friends who had worked on campaigns with her were supportive — and worried. "People did say, 'I love this for you, but be safe,'" she recalled. "But ultimately this was something that is bigger than myself."

On the trail, she says her identity was both a help and a hindrance — and mostly, it simply was not the subject. "It was probably a little bit of both," she said. "Whenever someone told me they voted for me and I asked them why they usually said something about the merits of the campaign. Being trans wasn't something that really came up. But I tried to build a broad coalition and the people who didn't vote for me probably weren't going to vote for me anyway."

That is worth sitting with. In a state where trans people have been turned into a campaign issue by the highest offices in the land, a trans woman knocked on doors in one of Orange County's most conservative corners, talked about soil and water and farmworker pay — and won. The voters who chose her chose her for the campaign she ran. The history books will remember the first; the neighbors will remember the knock on the door.

Why Florida makes this a first that matters

Florida is not a neutral backdrop for this story. Under Governor Ron DeSantis, the state has passed some of the harshest anti-LGBTQ+ laws in the country: restrictions on classroom instruction about sexual orientation and gender identity — the law widely known as "Don't Say Gay" — restrictions on gender-affirming care for minors, restrictions on trans people's access to bathrooms, and a law barring transgender students from girls' and women's athletics.

The campaign cycle that carried Pérez to office was also the most expensive anti-trans advertising blitz in American history. Republicans spent more than $400 million on anti-LGBTQ+ advertising nationally in the 2026 midterms, including about $187 million aimed specifically at trans people — a figure we covered here on November 13, when election-eve polling showed voters were already calling the obsession strange and over the top. In Florida, anti-trans forces ran an estimated $200 million worth of transphobic ads during the cycle, according to reporting on Pérez's own post-election briefing. She raised around $6,000. "We still defeated that rhetoric," she said.

Equality Florida's executive director, Stratton Pollitzer, put the meaning of the win in one sentence: "In a state whose right wing politicians have built their brands by trying to bully transgender people out of public life, Quinnie Perez's election is a defiant declaration that we deserve better. She shattered a ceiling and is paving the way for other transgender leaders to continue to rise."

Part of a wave, not a one-off

Pérez's win was one of the firsts we wrote about here on November 6, when at least eleven trans and nonbinary candidates won their races across the country on election night. The wave kept building after that post: in Alabama, Allison Montgomery won a race to become the state's first transgender state lawmaker. In Washington, Jaelynn Scott became the state's first openly trans lawmaker. And here in this blog on November 22, we covered Rep. Sarah McBride's election to House Democratic leadership — the first trans person ever elected to leadership in Congress.

That is the pattern to notice. The firsts are no longer isolated. They are stacking up, from a soil and water board in Orange County to a statehouse in Montgomery to the leadership table in Washington — and they are happening in the same cycle as the most expensive anti-trans advertising campaign ever run. The ads said trans people do not belong in public life. The voters keep answering otherwise.

None of this means the fight is over, and this post will not pretend otherwise. A seat on a conservation district does not repeal a single Florida law. It does not restore gender-affirming care for trans kids in the state, or reopen a bathroom, or take a trans student off the bench. What it does is put a trans woman in a room where decisions get made — even small decisions, about water and soil and the people who work the land — and it proves, in the state that tried hardest to make trans people invisible, that voters will still choose one to represent them.

What this means for trans health

This blog is a trans health blog, and a soil and water board might seem far from a clinic. It is not. Pérez's platform is a public health platform: farmworker safety and fair pay, housing security, clean water for drinking and food production, wetlands that buffer communities against storms. Farmworkers — disproportionately immigrants, disproportionately people of color — face some of the worst health conditions of any workforce in America: heat, pesticide exposure, injury, and almost no access to care. A board member who has worked beside them, and who ran on their safety, is a health advocate in the room where those decisions are made.

And her own story is a health story, too. Depression and burnout before she understood herself; feeling healthier and "more like myself" after she did. That is what gender-affirming care and living openly actually look like in a real person's life — not a headline, not a talking point, just a woman who feels more like herself and then goes out at 4 a.m. to work for her neighbors.

Representation is not a cure-all. But a trans person in office changes what the next trans kid in Florida believes is possible, and it changes what the next trans adult believes is worth trying. Pérez ran because it was, in her words, "bigger than myself." The result is bigger than one seat, too: the first openly trans person elected to public office in Florida — a farmworker, a daughter of immigrants, a woman who won the most conservative corner of her county by talking about water, soil, and the people who work the land.

She takes office in January. The board has no budget and no staff. It has a new member who knows how to show up at 4 a.m. Watch what she does with it.

What to watch next: Pérez takes her seat in January 2027 — watch whether the board's planned November fundraiser and her push to raise the district's profile give it real reach for the first time in years. And watch the other firsts from this cycle: Allison Montgomery in Alabama, Jaelynn Scott in Washington, and Sarah McBride in congressional leadership, as the 120th Congress convenes on January 3, 2027.
