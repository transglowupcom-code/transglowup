Universe, Meet Zyri: The Voice of Steven Universe Just Came Out as Trans — and for the First Time, She Can See Her Future

On Wednesday, November 25 — the eve of Thanksgiving — the voice of one of the queerest cartoons ever made came back after eight years away and said the thing she had been carrying the whole time: she's trans. Zyri's letter is long, honest, and it ends happy. On a day built around gratitude, that's worth sitting with.

November 27, 2026 · 6 min read

"Hey stranger, been a while"

Loves, here is how it happened. On Wednesday, Zyri posted a handful of smiling photos to her Instagram account — one of her grinning ear to ear, one at a light show, one of her hand intertwined with another hand, both wearing bejeweled wedding bands — and wrote, in her own words: "hey stranger, been a while. i wrote you a letter if you want to read it. it's in my bio, but it's really long so, tl;dr…i'm alive, i'm 'back', and i'm t-r-a-n-s. surpriiiiiiiiiise."

The letter itself, titled simply "A Letter," lives on her newly founded Substack. It opens with a warning, and we will borrow it: it covers abuse and mental health struggles, and anyone who would rather skip that part should. But Zyri also tells you up front where it lands: "This is something I've wanted to write for most of my adult life," she writes. "Please know that I'm deeply grateful, and though it's been a long and winding road, this story does have a happy ending."

She goes by Zyri now — a mononym, no surname — and uses she/her pronouns. That is the headline, and it is also the least of it. The letter is about everything that came before the headline, and everything that came after.

The voice we grew up with

If you watched cartoons in the 2010s, you already know her voice, even if you didn't know her name. Zyri began acting at age seven, landed her first role in 2009 on the online sitcom Diary of a Single Mom, and went on to appear on Hannah Montana, iCarly, and The Goldbergs. Then came the role that made her a star: the voice of Steven, the young half-human, half-gem hero of Cartoon Network's Steven Universe, which first aired in 2013.

For a whole generation of queer and trans kids, Steven Universe was not just a cartoon. At the time of its run it was widely considered the queerest cartoon on television, filled with LGBTQ+ characters who spoke openly about their queerness and transness, in a show made for all ages. Fans wrote essays about what it meant to them. Trans fans in particular claimed it, because a show about a boy raised by magical women who literally fuse — two beings becoming one new person, together — was speaking a language a lot of us already knew by heart.

And all the while, the person giving Steven his voice was carrying something she couldn't say out loud yet. In her letter, Zyri writes that coming to terms with her dysphoria and her transness was "only a small piece of my journey towards being a whole, authentic person, but it was notable as the first thing that truly felt like mine in my entire life." Read that sentence again. For a child performer — someone whose voice, face, and schedule belonged to other people from the age of seven — the first thing that ever felt like hers was herself.

The eight quiet years

Zyri stepped away from public life about eight years ago, and the letter explains why, plainly and without softening it. Her mental health was unraveling during the final season of recording Steven Universe. "As the 2010s came to a close, my complete unraveling was well underway, and I steadily lost all my ability to live a functional life," she writes. "One by one, I stopped working, auditioning, returning messages from friends and loved ones, going outside, or sleeping normally." She came to realize she had "no understanding of life besides throwing myself into my work as an entertainer."

In the years that followed, she was diagnosed as autistic. She was sexually harassed, stalked, and doxxed by fans. She was sexually abused by intimate partners and groomed by a mentor figure. It came to a crescendo, she writes, when she was repeatedly, violently physically abused by a partner she was living with. We are not going to linger on those details — they are hers to tell, and she has told them in her own words for anyone who wants to read them. But it matters that we say them plainly, because they are part of why this coming-out took eight years, and why the fact that she is here, smiling in those photos, is not a small thing.

She also feared being outed in the early days of her transition, and losing control of her own story before she was ready to tell it. So she left Los Angeles, and she waited until the story was hers to tell, on her own terms, in her own time. "I count myself extremely lucky to be alive and well," she writes, reflecting on child stardom. "Many of us no longer are."

A happy ending, on her terms

Here is the part to hold onto. After accepting her gender identity, Zyri writes, "now, for the very first time, I can envision the rest of my life." She is surrounded now by what she calls a loving group of people she calls family. Through what she describes as a serendipitous chance meeting, she found the love of her life in a dear friend — and this year, the two of them celebrated one year of being, in her words, "blissfully, joyously gay married." The wedding bands in the photo say the rest: a blue gemstone and a pink one, surrounded by diamonds.

She is 29 now — she writes about celebrating her twenty-eighth birthday a little over a year ago, and what it meant to survive past twenty-seven, an age she had once, in her words, cynically viewed as a cosmically assigned expiration date. "What twenty-one year old me would've cynically viewed as being past my cosmically-assigned expiration date, is now something that truly feels worth celebrating," she writes. She is cautiously looking at returning to performing — she says she genuinely loves and misses it, and hopes to make music again. She self-released the album A Picture Perfect Hollywood Heartbreak in 2018, sang on all four Steven Universe original cast recordings, and last voiced Robin in the 2024 animated film Justice League: Crisis on Infinite Earths.

But she is also clear about the boundary: she places an extremely high value on privacy these days, and for the foreseeable future she intends to engage publicly only when it is necessary. That is not a door closing. That is a person who spent her childhood performing for other people deciding, finally, that her life belongs to her. The response, so far, has been exactly what it should be. The official Cartoon Network account commented on her post: "You are loved." Model Vivian Wilson wrote, "Welcome to the community!"

Why this one lands on Thanksgiving

It is not an accident that this story feels like a Thanksgiving story, even though nobody planned it that way. Zyri posted on the eve of the holiday, and we are writing about it on the day itself — a day that, for a lot of trans people, is complicated. Some of us are sitting at tables where we are loved and fully ourselves. Some of us are sitting at tables where we are tolerated, or misgendered, or quiet. Some of us are not going home at all.

Zyri's letter speaks to all three of those tables, because it is, at its heart, a story about chosen family. The family she has now — the loving group of people she calls family, the wife she met by chance, the friends who helped her make it this far — is the family she built after the life she was handed stopped working. "I truly couldn't have done it without you," she writes to the friends and followers who stayed. That is what gratitude actually looks like when you have had to build it from scratch: not a performance of thankfulness, but a name for the people who kept you alive.

And there is something else here that is worth naming on a day like today. Coming out has no deadline. Zyri was the voice of a beloved show, and she still needed eight quiet years, an autism diagnosis, a move out of Los Angeles, a marriage, and a whole new life before she could say this in public. Nobody is late to their own truth. If you are reading this and you are still figuring it out, or you have figured it out and haven't said it yet, or you said it years ago and are still waiting for the world to catch up — you are not behind. You are on your own schedule, and it counts.

If today is hard, you are not alone in that either. Our hub's support and crisis resources are linked from the home page, and the people there are there for exactly this kind of day.

Meanwhile, in care access: a new free clinic opens in Massachusetts

While we are talking about good news, here is a piece of it from the care side. Project Beacon, a new Massachusetts nonprofit, began enrolling patients on Monday, November 16, offering free telehealth gender-affirming care — hormone prescriptions and puberty blockers from a part-time physician, with priority for young people whose programs have closed. It refuses all federal funding, relying instead on private donations and some state money, so that federal restrictions cannot reach it. Its first-year budget is about $200,000, mostly from private donations, and it has asked Massachusetts' Affirming Health Care Trust Fund for $75,000 in start-up support. The nonprofit is fiscally sponsored by the Massachusetts chapter of the National Organization for Women, and its interim director, Steph deNormand, also directs the trans care program at Fenway Health.

The honest limits, because they matter: the appointments are free, but patients still have to pay for their prescriptions, labs, and care from other providers. You do not have to live in Massachusetts, but you do have to be physically in the state for each telehealth appointment. And one part-time clinician, serving up to 200 patients a month, is a lifeline, not a replacement for the programs that closed — 17-year-old Max Nash, who lost his care when Fenway Health closed its teen program last year, told WBUR the project is "awesome" but that it "won't be able to fill the gap created by the institutions that cut their programs." It is still better than nothing, he said. We agree — and we will keep watching whether more clinics follow this model. Baystate Health, Massachusetts' largest health system in the west of the state, also announced this week that it is resuming youth gender-affirming care, as we reported on Tuesday.
