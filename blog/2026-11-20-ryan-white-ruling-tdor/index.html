A Reagan-Appointed Judge Just Blocked the Ban on Trans HIV Care — On the Day We Remember the Ones We Lost

Yesterday, a federal judge in Boston did something the government has spent two years trying to prevent: he said, in plain language, that a doctor may not be punished for calling a trans patient by their name. And he did it on the eve of Transgender Day of Remembrance — the day we say the names of the trans people we lost this year. Loves, hold both of those things together today. A court said trans lives count, in the clinic and in the law. And tonight, we will still light candles for the ones who are gone.

November 20, 2026 · 9 min read

What happened on Thursday

On November 19, 2026, U.S. District Judge William G. Young of the District of Massachusetts issued a 165-page ruling striking down the Trump administration's restrictions on HIV funding for transgender patients. The case is American Academy of HIV Medicine et al. v. Health Resources and Services Administration et al., and it was decided on the merits after a full bench trial on September 23, 2026 — not a preliminary order, which gives the ruling more weight than an early injunction.

The restrictions at issue were funding conditions imposed on the Ryan White HIV/AIDS Program. Beginning in March 2026, the Health Resources and Services Administration — the division of the Department of Health and Human Services that runs the program — added conditions designed to carry out the president's executive order directing the federal government to recognize only two sexes, male and female. In June 2026, federal officials issued funding notices barring the use of program funds for gender-affirming medical care for transgender people living with HIV. The conditions required providers to ensure funding would support only activities using "sex-based definitions grounded in biological reality" and not "social or medical experimentation."

Lambda Legal and Herbert Smith Freehills Kramer filed the lawsuit on June 10, 2026, on behalf of the American Academy of HIV Medicine, the HIV Medicine Association, the International Association of Providers of AIDS Care, and two individual providers. At the end of the September trial, Judge Young urged the administration to settle the case. The administration rejected the plaintiffs' settlement proposal, according to an October 2 court filing — and so the judge decided it himself.

What the judge actually said

Judge Young found that the funding conditions violated the First Amendment, because they were designed to suppress what the administration considered to be "dangerous ideas" — penalizing doctors for acknowledging that transgender people exist and for referring to their patients by their preferred pronouns. He also found that the restrictions amounted to unconstitutional discrimination on the basis of sex, in violation of the Fifth Amendment.

And then he said the part that will be quoted for years. "They are based in animus, loathing, and discrimination," he wrote. "They do not stand for justice — in fact, they defy it. They are contrary to the laws of this nation and cannot continue to stand."

It matters who wrote those words. Judge Young was appointed by Republican President Ronald Reagan. This was not a ruling from a judge the administration can dismiss as an ideological opponent. It was a ruling from a Reagan appointee, after a full trial, who looked at the government's own record and found that its policy was built on hostility toward trans people — and said so, in the court's own voice.

Jose Abrigo, a lawyer for the plaintiffs at Lambda Legal, called the ruling a "sweeping merits victory for the plaintiffs." The court declared the conditions unlawful, vacated them, and permanently blocked the conditions — or any similar policies — from being imposed against the members of the plaintiff associations, which cover virtually every HIV care provider in the United States. The Department of Health and Human Services did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

Why this program, and why it matters this much

To understand what was at stake, you have to understand what the Ryan White HIV/AIDS Program is. Congress created it in 1990, and it is the largest federal program focused on HIV care in the country — the safety net for low-income and uninsured people living with HIV. More than half of all people with diagnosed HIV in the United States receive services through it: more than 576,000 people in 2023, and over 90 percent of Ryan White clients receiving HIV medical care were virally suppressed that year, far above the national average.

For trans people living with HIV, the program is more than a funding stream. Nearly half of the American Academy of HIV Medicine's members rely on Ryan White funding, and almost half of those members provide gender-affirming medical care — care that has historically been part of the program's own framework of comprehensive services. Lambda Legal has put it plainly: for many trans women with HIV, gender-affirming medical care is what keeps them connected to their doctors and to their HIV treatment. Take the affirming care away, and people do not just lose hormones — they lose the clinic, the relationship, and the treatment that keeps them alive.

That is what the March conditions did. They forced providers into an impossible choice: keep the federal funding that pays for their patients' HIV care, or keep treating their trans patients as the people they are. The judge's ruling ends that choice. Providers can once again treat transgender patients based on medical need, consistent with best practices, and without discrimination.

What Thursday does not do

Now the honest part, because a win this big deserves to be described exactly. Thursday's ruling is a trial-court decision, and the government can appeal it. Nothing in the ruling stops the administration from trying again through a different route, and the broader crackdown it was part of — the military ban, the passport policy, the restrictions on care for trans people in federal prisons — is still in force. The court fight over those policies is running in other courtrooms, including the Supreme Court, which will decide on December 4 whether to take up the troops case we covered yesterday.

But a merits ruling after a full trial is harder to undo than an injunction, and this one was written to be hard to ignore: 165 pages, a Reagan appointee, and a finding of animus stated in the open. For now, the funding conditions are vacated, and the providers who care for trans people living with HIV can do their jobs without choosing between their patients and their funding. That is real, and it is worth saying so plainly.

Today is Transgender Day of Remembrance

And now, loves, the other half of today. November 20 is Transgender Day of Remembrance — the 27th observance. It began in 1999, when advocate Gwendolyn Ann Smith organized a vigil for Rita Hester, a Black trans woman murdered in Boston in 1998. What started as one vigil in one city is now observed around the world, and the list of names read at those vigils keeps getting longer.

The numbers released for this year's observance are these. TGEU's Trans Murder Monitoring project recorded 273 trans and gender-diverse people reported murdered worldwide between October 1, 2025, and September 30, 2026 — on average, one murder every 32 hours, across 35 countries. Ninety-five percent of the victims were trans women or transfeminine people, and three-quarters were Black or Brown trans people. Twenty-nine of them were killed here in the United States. The project has now recorded more than 5,500 murders since it began counting in 2009, and it is careful to say these are only the murders that were reported.

Here at home, Advocates for Trans Equality's 2026 Remembrance Report honors 62 known trans people who died in the United States between November 2025 and October 2026. Of those, 28 were killed, 21 died by suicide, and 13 died of natural or unknown causes. Of the 28 who were killed, about 57 percent — 16 people — were Black, including 14 Black trans women. A4TE is clear that the count is almost certainly incomplete: police, news outlets, and families often misname or misgender trans people after they die, so many deaths are never even recorded as the deaths of trans people.

Say a few of their names with us. La'Daijah Walters, 32. Ashtyn Tice, 17. Kalena "Peaches" Croskey, 32. Summer Devi Mehta, 17. Jonathan "Jon" McKinsey, 40. They were not statistics. They were people with lives, families, and futures — and the full list, with their stories, is kept at the Trans Remembrance Project's memorial.

Why these two stories belong together

It is not a coincidence that the ruling came the day before Remembrance. It is the same week, the same fight, seen from two sides. Transgender Awareness Week gave us a federal appeals court protecting 27 trans troops, Australia's top health body backing puberty blockers, Delhi opening its hospitals to trans families — and, alongside all of it, the bans, the medical boards, and now a list of 273 names.

The ruling and the remembrance are about the same thing: whether trans people get to be seen — by a doctor, by a court, by a country — as the people they are. The judge in Boston said the government cannot punish a clinician for seeing a trans patient clearly. The vigils tonight say the same thing in a different language: we will not let the dead be unseen, either. Care is a form of remembrance. Remembrance is a form of care.

So today, do both. If you get your HIV care through a Ryan White provider, ask them what Thursday's ruling means for your care — the conditions that forced them to choose are gone. And tonight, find a vigil. Advocates for Trans Equality's Trans Remembrance Project hosts its annual virtual vigil this evening from 6 to 7 p.m. Eastern, and vigils are being held in communities across the country. Light a candle. Say a name out loud. And then, tomorrow, keep going — because the ruling is real, the dead are real, and the work of this week is holding both at once.

What you can do next: If you receive HIV care through a Ryan White provider, Thursday's ruling means your provider can once again offer gender-affirming care without risking its federal funding — ask your clinic what changes for you. Tonight, join a Transgender Day of Remembrance vigil in your community, or the Trans Remembrance Project's virtual vigil from 6 to 7 p.m. Eastern.
