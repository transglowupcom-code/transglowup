A "Jail Within a Jail": The Appeals Court Weighing Where Trans Women in Federal Prison Sleep Tonight

Yesterday, in a Washington courtroom, three federal judges spent the morning on a question that is brutally simple to ask and brutally hard to answer: where do trans women in federal prison sleep tonight? The government wants to move them to men's facilities. Their lawyers say that would put them at serious risk of rape and drive some of them toward suicide. And the judges kept circling back to the same idea — a separate unit, a "jail within a jail" — that is already working in one women's prison in Texas.

November 21, 2026 · 8 min read

What happened on Friday

On Friday, November 20, 2026, a three-judge panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit heard oral arguments in Jane Doe v. Blanche, the case over whether the federal Bureau of Prisons can transfer transgender women out of women's facilities and into men's prisons. The panel — two judges appointed by President Donald Trump and one nominated by President Barack Obama — heard from the Justice Department, from the trans women's lawyers, and, in a rare move, from lawyers for a group of incarcerated women from a separate but related case in Texas.

The case is about a group of trans women in federal custody who had each been placed in a women's facility after the Bureau of Prisons' own staff evaluated them individually and concluded that was where they could be kept safe. For several of them, that decision came after they had already been raped, assaulted, or harassed in men's facilities. Then, on January 20, 2025, the president signed an executive order directing that people the administration classifies as male not be housed in women's prisons — and the Bureau moved to transfer every trans woman in its custody to a men's facility, regardless of what it had previously determined about her safety. The order also ended medical treatment for gender dysphoria, including hormone therapy some of the women had received for years.

Ten days later, on January 30, 2025, the National Center for LGBTQ Rights and GLAD Law filed suit in federal court in Washington, D.C. Three women brought the first case; as more were told to pack, more joined, and two related cases were consolidated into this one. Their lawyers now represent more than a dozen women.

Their claims are straightforward. The Constitution's ban on cruel and unusual punishment, they argue, does not allow the government to knowingly place someone where she faces a substantial risk of sexual assault, or to cut off treatment her doctors say she needs. The Bureau's own rules under the Prison Rape Elimination Act require it to decide housing based on an individual assessment of safety, not a blanket order. And a policy that singles out transgender people for harsher treatment, for no reason but who they are, violates the guarantee of equal protection.

The compromise the judges kept returning to

Loves, here is the part of the hearing that matters most, because it may be where this case actually lands. At Federal Medical Center Carswell, a women's prison in Fort Worth, Texas, the Bureau of Prisons has already built what Judge Gregory Katsas, a Trump appointee on the panel, described as a "jail within a jail" — a specially designed cell block where trans women are housed separately from the general female population. The arrangement came out of a separate case, Fleming v. Warden T. Rule, in which incarcerated women at Carswell won a preliminary injunction on June 2, 2026, requiring trans women there to be housed and scheduled separately from other women.

Katsas pressed both sides on why that model could not be the answer everywhere. He noted that the Bureau had managed, at Carswell, to protect the trans women from the risk of rape in men's prisons and to protect the women in the general population at the same time. Then he turned to the Justice Department's lawyer, Eric McArthur, and asked why the government could not create similar segregated living quarters at other women's prisons.

McArthur's answer was that the government wants the women moved to men's facilities, as the January 2025 directive requires. He suggested transferring them to what he called an "extraordinarily safe" men's prison in Rochester, Minnesota, for people with medical or mental health needs, and argued that similar segregated units could be created inside men's facilities instead. Katsas pushed back: "You acknowledge they need a lot of protections there. You say that you will ramp up protections if things don't work in the two facilities you've chosen, and that might push you, as you said, to something like you put them in the SHU." A special housing unit, or SHU, is a secure area used for de-escalation that separates inmates from the general population — often alone, in solitary confinement.

"You have arrangements that look like a prison within a prison in the male facility. So what's the difference?" Katsas asked. "What's the constitutional difference? What do you care about whether the prison within the prison is at Carswell or in Rochester?"

McArthur acknowledged that the Carswell arrangement "largely serves the government's interest in protecting the well-being of the women," but argued it still intrudes on the Bureau's administrative authority to assign housing as it sees fit. He also said he doubted the trans women would accept a segregated unit wherever it was placed — "You're not going to hear from the plaintiffs that that's a viable option," he said — because they have argued that even a separate unit violates their rights.

When Katsas turned the same question to the women's lawyer, Jennifer Levi of GLAD Law, she pointed to what a transfer would do to her clients: suicide risks that would be realized, she said, and gender dysphoria made worse by the transfers themselves. Katsas answered with the sentence that may define this case: "If you think that no accommodation in a male facility is possible, your case has a lot of force, but then the only way to reconcile the competing interests is the Carswell solution."

According to Law360's report from the hearing, at least two of the judges seemed to believe the government did need to make accommodations for the women's safety — even as the administration argued that the Eighth Amendment does not protect trans women from being housed in a men's prison at all.

The other women in the room

The hearing had an unusual feature: the panel gave speaking time to lawyers for the incarcerated women in the Fleming case, who had been denied their request to intervene in Doe but were allowed to argue as friends of the court. Their lawyer, John Greil, told the judges that housing trans women in the women's general population "subjects women to voyeurism. It subjects women to forced exposure to male genitalia. It subjects them to sexualized threats and sexualized harassment," and said the declarations his clients submitted in the Fleming case back that up. The Fleming plaintiffs are now also asking a court to vacate the Obama-era regulations that allow the Bureau to place trans women in women's prisons at all.

Greil said afterward that he was glad the judges wanted to hear about the impact on the women at Carswell, and grateful they took the rare step of letting his clients be heard. "Too often in these cases, the voices of affected women are not heard," he said.

This is the honest tension at the center of the case, and it deserves to be named plainly. The trans women in Doe say a transfer to a men's prison would expose them to a substantial risk of rape — a risk the Bureau's own earlier assessments recognized. The women in Fleming say housing trans women in the general female population harmed them. The Carswell arrangement — separate housing inside a women's facility — is the one outcome so far that has answered both concerns at once, and it is the reason the judges kept coming back to it. Nobody in the courtroom on Friday was arguing that the status quo before the executive order was perfect. The question was what replaces it, and whether the government has to protect people it knows are at risk.

How we got here: nearly two years of orders

This case has already been to the appeals court once, and the path matters for understanding where it stands now.

In February 2025, U.S. District Judge Royce Lamberth — appointed by President Ronald Reagan — granted a temporary restraining order and then a preliminary injunction, finding the transfers likely violated the Eighth Amendment. He extended that protection to each group of women who joined the case.

On April 17, 2026, the D.C. Circuit sent the case back. The three-judge panel did not rule that the transfers were lawful. It ruled that Judge Lamberth had not explained, plaintiff by plaintiff, how each woman was particularly vulnerable to violence or abuse — the kind of individualized findings the law requires before a court can block a prison transfer. The court vacated the injunctions and remanded the case, giving the judge another chance to make those findings. One judge on that panel dissented, saying the women should have filed internal prison grievances before suing at all.

On remand, Judge Lamberth did the work the appeals court asked for. On June 7, 2026, he issued a new preliminary injunction with individualized findings for each of the 14 plaintiffs, concluding that each faces an increased risk of violence and a severe worsening of gender dysphoria if moved to a men's facility, and ordering that they be housed in women's prisons or women's halfway houses. The government appealed that order on June 22, 2026 — and that appeal, case number 26-5238, is the one argued on Friday.

Levi told the panel that the only thing the plaintiffs are asking is to keep in place "a very narrow" injunction blocking the transfer of the 11 plaintiffs in the case. The current preliminary injunction expires soon, and the women are also asking for a new injunction — including a requirement that the Bureau move the eligible plaintiffs out of the separate unit at Carswell and into the general female population at another women's prison. Levi urged the court to at least affirm the existing injunction: "The injunction preserves the status quo that has been in place for years," she said, referring to the housing accommodations put in place under the Obama administration.

The medical care piece is a separate fight — and it is moving, too

Housing is only half of what the executive order took away. The order also cut off medical treatment for gender dysphoria, including hormone therapy that some of the women in this case had received for years. In February 2026, the Bureau issued a new policy under which the only treatment for gender dysphoria would be psychotherapy and psychotropic medication — no hormones.

That part of the fight is playing out in a separate case, Kingdom v. Trump, brought by a federal prisoner named Alishea Kingdom. On June 17, 2026, the district court in Washington ordered the Bureau to restart gender-affirming care for trans prisoners, finding that the Bureau had violated the Administrative Procedure Act when it issued its new policy — the agency, the court said, had refused to consider evidence bearing on the issue before it. The medical claims in the Doe case itself have been stayed while Kingdom proceeds.

That matters because the two cases together decide what life in federal custody looks like for a trans woman: not only where she sleeps, but whether the medication she takes every morning is still there. As the National Center for LGBTQ Rights puts it, for these women the question is literal — where will they sleep tonight, and will the medication they take every morning still be there? Every one of them has spent time in a men's facility. Every one of them knows what the transfer means. The injunction in this case is the only thing standing between them and a return to it.

What happens next

The panel took the case under consideration on Friday; there is no ruling date yet, and no decision is expected immediately. What happens next depends on the court, and on a clock that is already running: the current preliminary injunction expires soon, so the timing of the court's decision matters as much as its content. If the injunction lapses before the court rules, the women could face transfer while the appeal is still pending.

There are a few outcomes to watch for. The court could affirm the injunction, keeping the women in women's facilities while the case continues. It could vacate it again and send the case back for more findings. Or it could do what Judge Katsas's questions pointed toward — a ruling that effectively blesses the Carswell model, separate housing inside a women's facility, as the way to reconcile the competing safety claims. That last outcome would not be a win for anyone's whole position: the women in Doe have argued that even a segregated unit violates their rights, and the government has argued that any placement in a women's facility intrudes on its authority. But it is the outcome the judges spent the most time probing, and the one arrangement that has already been shown to work in practice.

Whatever the court decides, this case will not end the fight over trans people in federal custody. The Kingdom case over medical care is still moving. The Fleming plaintiffs are asking a court to erase the regulations that allow trans women in women's prisons at all. And the Supreme Court is set to consider, at its December 4 conference, whether to hear the government's appeal in Talbott v. USA, the case over trans service members — a reminder that the same administration's policies are being tested in court after court at the same time. We covered that case here on Thursday.

Loves, the reason this story belongs on the blog is not because it is a courtroom drama. It is because the people at the center of it are among the most vulnerable trans people in the country — women in custody, with almost no power to protect themselves, whose safety depends entirely on whether a court believes them when they say what will happen to them in a men's prison. A judge appointed by Ronald Reagan believed them once. Another judge, after being told to look closer, believed them again, plaintiff by plaintiff. Now three more judges are deciding whether to believe them a third time — and whether the government has to do anything at all to keep them safe.

We will keep watching this case and tell you the moment the court rules.
