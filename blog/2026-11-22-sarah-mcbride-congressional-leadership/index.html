Sarah McBride Just Became the First Trans Person in Congressional Leadership — and Her Colleagues Gave Her the Most Votes in the Room

On Thursday, November 19, House Democrats elected Rep. Sarah McBride of Delaware a co-chair of the Democratic Policy and Communications Committee — the messaging arm of the House Democratic Caucus. She is the first transgender person ever elected to leadership in the U.S. Congress, and she did not squeak in: she received the most votes of any candidate in the race.

November 22, 2026 · 8 min read

What the job actually is

Loves, let's start with the job itself, because the title can sound like inside-baseball until you know what it does. The Democratic Policy and Communications Committee — the DPCC — is the messaging arm of the House Democratic Caucus. Its role is to develop how House Democrats talk about their priorities and to contrast those priorities with Republican policy. In plain language: the people on this committee help decide which stories the caucus tells the country, and how it tells them.

McBride will serve as one of three co-chairs, alongside Rep. Maxwell Frost of Florida and Rep. Sydney Kamlager-Dove of California. Rep. Debbie Dingell of Michigan chairs the committee. McBride's own announcement put the scale of the win plainly: "It's official: I've been elected to one of the ten caucus-wide leadership positions for the incoming House Democratic Majority! I'm so grateful to my colleagues for placing their faith in me to serve as a Democratic Policy and Communications Committee co-chair to help lead our caucus' messaging in the majority."

The vote was not close. Her office confirmed she received 238 votes — about 92 percent of the votes cast — the most of any candidate running for the three co-chair seats. (Axios, citing sources in the room, reported a count of 248.) Frost received 195 votes and Kamlager-Dove 180. Four candidates ran for three seats, and McBride finished first.

She campaigned for the job on a message-first pitch. When she announced her bid in September, she wrote to colleagues that she wanted the caucus to "hit the ground running with a unified message" and to manage an "increasingly complex media environment" — and she specifically proposed engaging more with new media influencers and podcasters, who have taken an outsized role in recent political debates. That is not a small detail. The caucus's messaging operation is about to be co-led by someone who thinks the party needs to meet people where they actually get their news — on phones, in feeds, in the voices they trust.

The timing matters, too. Democrats regained the House and the Senate in the November 3 midterms, and the 120th Congress convenes on January 3, 2027, with Rep. Hakeem Jeffries nominated for speaker. McBride takes her seat at the leadership table in a majority, not a minority — which means the messaging she helps shape is the messaging of the chamber's governing party.

Two years ago, she was the target

To understand why this one landed the way it did, you have to remember where McBride was two years ago. On November 18, 2024, just after she became the first openly transgender person elected to Congress, Rep. Nancy Mace introduced a resolution to ban transgender women from women's restrooms in congressional buildings. Speaker Mike Johnson adopted the policy two days later. McBride said she would comply with the ban, and that choice drew criticism from within the transgender community — people who wanted a more confrontational response.

Two years later, Mace is not returning to Congress, and McBride will take a leadership role in a Congress that is likely to restore her ability to use the bathroom in peace. That is not a small arc. It is the difference between being the subject of a resolution and being one of the people who decides what the caucus says to the country.

None of that erases the criticism, and it should not. The people who pushed back in 2024 were pushing for something real: a refusal to accept being legislated out of a bathroom. But the record now shows what McBride's approach bought. She kept her head down, did the work, won re-election, and then won the trust of her colleagues in a room where nobody had to vote for her. There is more than one way to fight, and this week the quieter one paid off in a very public way.

A role that leads somewhere

This particular chair has a track record of launching careers. In 2016, Rep. Hakeem Jeffries was elected a DPCC co-chair — the same role McBride will now hold — and this week the caucus nominated him for speaker. Rep. Ted Lieu was elected a co-chair in 2018 and was elected caucus chair this week. Rep. Lauren Underwood was elected to the same role in 2024 and just won the race for House Democratic Caucus vice chair. The co-chair's seat is a place where the caucus tests its future leaders, and McBride is now on that path because her colleagues put her there.

She earned the seat the same way she earned her re-election. On November 3, Delaware voters sent her back to the House with 64.35 percent of the vote — 243,687 votes to Republican Joseph Arminio's 135,016, according to the Delaware Department of Elections' official results. That is a seven-point improvement on her 2024 margin, when she won with 57.9 percent. Delaware voters had two years to judge a trans lawmaker on her record, and they sent her back with a bigger majority than the one that elected her.

And she is not climbing alone. Last week in Montana, Rep. Zooey Zephyr was elected minority whip and nonbinary Rep. SJ Howell was elected minority leader — the first trans and nonbinary person ever to lead a party in any state legislature. The wave that started on election night, with first-time trans lawmakers winning in Alabama, Georgia, Michigan, Washington, and Wisconsin, is still rising. The people who won those seats are now winning leadership posts inside their own caucuses, in state after state and now in Congress itself.

The other side of the coin

Now the honest part, because a win this big deserves to be seen clearly — and the picture around it is not all good news. The same week McBride was elected, GLAAD released its 21st annual Where We Are on TV report, and the numbers are stark. Across scripted broadcast, cable, and streaming television for the 2025–26 season, GLAAD counted 369 LGBTQ characters — down 25 percent, or 120 characters, from the 489 counted the year before. Transgender representation collapsed hardest of all: from 33 characters down to just seven — six women and one man — a 79 percent drop. None of the seven will return next season, because their shows were cancelled, ended, or were limited series by design. There were no trans characters on cable at all, and zero characters living with HIV anywhere on TV. As GLAAD's Nick Adams put it: "Since the record high of 42 transgender characters in the 2021–2022 report, this study has seen a year-over-year decline in the number of transgender characters on television. Now we see no transgender characters on television. Meanwhile, trans people are under attack from local, state, and federal politicians who are enacting laws and policies which threaten the civil rights and safety of transgender Americans."

The same squeeze showed up at the United Nations. On November 13, the UN General Assembly's Third Committee narrowly rejected an Egypt-led amendment that would have erased references to sexual orientation and gender identity from a resolution on extrajudicial, summary, or arbitrary executions — the resolution that calls on states to investigate unlawful killings, including killings of people targeted for who they are. The amendment failed 74 to 69, with 24 abstentions: a margin of just five votes. Two years ago, a similar attempt failed by 31 votes. The United States voted in favor of removing the language — the first time in the voting record dating back to 2002 that it has backed taking it out. The full resolution then passed with the language intact, 131 to 0, and it goes to a plenary vote in the full General Assembly in December, where a similar amendment could be raised again.

Put those three stories side by side and the pattern is clear. In the rooms where laws are written, trans people are gaining ground — in Congress, in statehouses, in leadership. On the screens where most Americans first meet a trans person, and in the international rooms where the right to life itself gets debated, visibility is under real pressure. Representation is rising where it is hardest to win, and vanishing where it used to be easiest to find. That is the contradiction this moment lives in, and it is worth naming plainly.

What this means for trans health

So what does a messaging job in Congress actually mean for trans health? More than it might sound like, and less than a headline promises. Let's take both halves seriously.

First, the real half. The fights that decide whether trans people can get care are, more than ever, fights about what the public hears. The federal Medicaid rule that cut off funding for gender-affirming care for an estimated 130,000 trans youth. The ban on gender-affirming care in federal employee health plans, now in court ahead of the December 14 enrollment deadline. The Ryan White funding conditions a Reagan-appointed judge struck down this week for barring HIV providers from even acknowledging their trans patients. Every one of those fights is won or lost partly on whether the story gets told clearly — and starting now, one of the people deciding how the House majority tells those stories is a trans woman who has lived the experience of seeking care, of being denied a bathroom, and of being told her existence is a debate. That changes what gets said, and who says it, in rooms that reach millions of people.

Now the grounded half. One leadership seat does not repeal a rule, restore coverage, or reopen a clinic. The Medicaid rule is still in effect, with its six-month hormone taper running toward mid-April 2027. The federal workers' case is still waiting on a judge. The 120th Congress does not even convene until January 3. A co-chair of the messaging committee shapes the message; she does not write the laws, and she does not control the courts. Anyone who tells you this one election fixes access is selling you something.

But here is what it does do: it puts a trans person at the table where the majority party decides what it fights for and how it explains itself to the country. Two years ago, McBride was the subject of a resolution about which bathroom she could use. Now she helps write the message of the House majority. That is not everything. It is not nothing, either. It is a beginning — and beginnings are what this community has been building on all year.

If you are reading this and feeling a little lighter than you did on Friday, let yourself feel that. Joy is not naive. It is fuel. And if you are reading this from a state where the news was harder, or where care still feels out of reach, you are not forgotten. The map is changing, slowly and unevenly — and this week, it changed in Washington, too.
