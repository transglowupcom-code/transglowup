The Other Side of Tuesday: Colorado's New Bans — and Four States Where Repeal Is Now Possible

Tuesday, November 3, 2026, was a night of firsts for trans candidates, and Missouri voters said no to a care ban. But the same ballot carried losses, too: Colorado voters approved two measures restricting trans youth, and voters in three more states approved similar restrictions. Here is what those measures actually do, what happens next — and the part of the story that gives real hope.

November 7, 2026 · 6 min read

What Colorado voters approved

Loves, let's start with the facts, because the details matter more than the headlines. Colorado voters approved both Proposition 134 and Proposition 135 on Tuesday, though by relatively narrow margins, according to Colorado Public Radio. Here is what each one does:

  • Proposition 134 — school sports. It establishes new statewide rules requiring participation in school sports based on a student's sex assigned at birth. That means a trans girl would not be allowed to play on a girls' team, and a trans boy would not be allowed to play on a boys' team, in Colorado public schools.
  • Proposition 135 — surgery for minors. It prohibits certain surgeries on people under 18 when the purpose of the surgery is to alter the minor's biological sex characteristics. It would also prohibit state and federal funding, Medicaid reimbursement, and insurance coverage for those surgeries.

Both measures are scheduled to take effect on January 1, 2027. And here is a detail that matters for families trying to plan: Proposition 135 covers surgeries only. It does not ban puberty blockers or hormone therapy for minors — the measure was written to ban surgery while allowing hormone treatment to continue. That distinction will matter a lot in the months ahead, and it is worth knowing exactly, because panic thrives on vagueness and this post is not here to feed it.

It is also worth saying plainly what the data shows about the care at issue. Reporting on the election noted that top surgery is rare among minors, and bottom surgery on a minor is virtually nonexistent. The surgeries this measure bans were already uncommon. That does not make the ban harmless — for the small number of older teens and families it affects, it is a real loss, and the funding and insurance bans reach further than the surgery ban itself. But it does mean the measure was aimed at a rare procedure, not a widespread one, and the scale of what it changes should be understood honestly.

What happens next in Colorado

The vote is only the beginning of the story, because both measures leave big questions about how they will actually work — and the people charged with answering them do not all agree.

Proposition 134 does not establish a statewide process for determining a student's biological sex. Instead, school districts will have to develop their own policies, which could mean different approaches across Colorado. The measure does not assign the Colorado High School Activities Association a role in determining eligibility or enforcing the requirements, and it does not clearly spell out how the rules will be enforced at private schools or colleges. The Colorado Department of Education will have a role in addressing "intentional noncompliance" by public school districts, and the state commissioner of education can take remedial action if a district intentionally refuses to follow the law — but the measure does not spell out exactly how violations will be investigated or what specific consequences districts could face.

Enforcement of Proposition 135 will fall to professional licensing boards. The Colorado Department of Regulatory Agencies told CPR News that its licensing boards can set rules for implementing the measure and handle complaints about licensed professionals on a case-by-case basis. Depending on the findings, providers could face a formal warning, a fine, suspension, or loss of their license. The measure does not create a separate criminal penalty for violations, and the department did not identify a single board or agency that would oversee enforcement.

There is also political pushback coming from the top. Governor-elect Phil Weiser, who publicly opposed both propositions before the election, said on Wednesday that lawmakers should take another look at the measures and consider what adjustments might be appropriate. He specifically raised concerns about how Proposition 134 could be implemented, including the possibility of invasive examinations of student athletes. He has not outlined specific changes he would support — but a governor-elect publicly questioning a ballot measure's implementation is not nothing.

And the organizations that opposed the measures are not conceding. One Colorado, the state's LGBTQ advocacy organization, said it will "exhaust every option available" to work toward a Colorado where transgender students are allowed to be kids, not political symbols. In its statement after the election, the organization said: "To every transgender Coloradan reading this: You deserve dignity, care, opportunity, and love. No election result can take that away from you."

On the other side, Erin Lee, who led the campaigns behind both measures through her group Protect Kids Colorado, told CPR News the passage is a starting point, not an ending. Her organization is turning its attention to making sure the measures are carried out — and preparing for what she expects will be resistance from some schools and medical providers. "I'm so happy that the voters of Colorado have done the right thing, [but] there is still work to do," she said. "Protect Kids Colorado is a movement that's not going away."

It wasn't just Colorado

Colorado was not alone. Reuters reported that voters in four states — Washington, Colorado, Arizona, and Nebraska — approved ballot measures restricting transgender rights on Tuesday, even as Democrats swept to victory nationwide and retook control of Congress. That split result is the honest headline of the night: voters who backed Democratic candidates were still receptive to some of the arguments against trans inclusion, especially in youth sports.

Here is what passed elsewhere. In Arizona, voters expanded an existing ban on trans athletes to apply to essentially all sporting events, including private schools, club sports, and Little League, and banned trans people from using their gender-aligned bathrooms and locker rooms at sporting events. In Nebraska, voters approved a sports ban and enshrined it in the state constitution, which makes it far harder to undo than an ordinary law. In Washington, a measure requiring athletes to play on teams matching their sex at birth — and requiring those seeking to play on female teams to submit documentation from a healthcare provider verifying their sex — passed with more than 56 percent of the vote.

The scale of what already existed matters, too. Twenty-seven states had laws in place before Tuesday restricting trans participation in youth sports, according to the Movement Advancement Project. And public opinion on sports is genuinely divided: a Reuters/Ipsos poll in April found that 67 percent of survey respondents supported banning transgender people from competing in female school sports — 92 percent of Republicans, but only 44 percent of Democrats. Loves, we are not going to pretend that number does not exist. It does, and it is the reason sports bans keep passing even in states that elect Democrats. The work of changing minds on this issue is real, unfinished work, and it will not be done by one election.

But the same ballot also carried defeats for the other side. In Washington, voters rejected a measure that would have expanded parental access to school records, potentially enabling parents to learn about a student's gender identity against the child's will. And in Missouri, as we covered yesterday, voters rejected Amendment 3, which would have written a ban on gender-affirming care for minors into the state constitution. The night was mixed — not a sweep in either direction.

The other direction: four states where repeal is now possible

Now for the part of the story that has not gotten enough attention. The same election that brought new restrictions in some states also shifted the balance of power enough that youth care bans could actually be repealed in four others: Arizona, Missouri, North Carolina, and Texas. That analysis comes from Erin in the Morning, and it is worth walking through state by state — with the honest caveat that none of it is guaranteed.

  • Arizona — the clearest path. Democrats are set to take control of both chambers of the Arizona Legislature for the first time in decades, with substantial majorities. Governor Katie Hobbs, who won reelection Tuesday, has vetoed several anti-transgender bills. Arizona has one of the oldest gender-affirming care bans on the books — a ban on surgery for transgender youth, covering top surgery sometimes sought by older trans teens — and a simple majority can repeal it, which would make Arizona the first state to legislatively remove a gender-affirming care ban. The catch: the legislature cannot overturn the newly passed sports and bathroom ban, which would require a court fight or a future referendum.
  • Missouri — the clock is already ticking. Missouri's ban on gender-affirming care for trans youth sunsets in August 2027, meaning the limits on puberty blockers and hormones naturally expire unless Republicans renew them. Democrats have broken the GOP supermajorities in the state legislature, giving them more leverage to filibuster and disrupt attempts to remove the sunset clause. With enough maneuvering, Democrats could ensure that gender-affirming medication for trans youth becomes legal in the state again — without having to pass anything at all.
  • North Carolina — one seat away. North Carolina Democrats are currently one Senate seat short of a trifecta, and the remaining seat is likely headed to a recount. If Democrats capture that seat, they could remove the ban on gender-affirming care for transgender youth, as well as the state's "Don't Say Gay or Trans" law and its sports ban. Because Democrats hold the governorship, they would not need supermajorities to pass bills removing the bans.
  • Texas — the long road through the courts. In 2024, the entirely Republican Texas Supreme Court upheld the state's gender-affirming care ban — but the decision was 8–1, not unanimous, with Justice Debra Lehrmann dissenting and calling the ban "cruel" and unconstitutional. Four Democrats were just elected to that court, making a 5–4 majority possible alongside Lehrmann. Cases could soon begin making their way up through the state courts, potentially challenging everything from the bathroom ban to the care ban. Courts move slowly, and this is the longest road of the four — but it is a road that did not exist a week ago.

None of this is a given. Republicans can still force a vote in Missouri. North Carolina's repeal depends on a recount going Democrats' way. Arizona will test whether Democrats are actually willing to repeal a surgery ban now that they have the votes. And Texas runs through the courts, which can take years. But after Tuesday, for the first time, trans people in these four states have a hope that did not exist a week ago — and hope, backed by a real legislative or legal path, is not the same as wishful thinking.

What this means for trans health access

So where does all of this leave trans health, the day after a night that pulled in both directions at once? In an honest, complicated place.

On one side, access got narrower this week. Colorado's surgery ban takes effect January 1, 2027, and its sports ban will push trans kids out of school teams in a state that had been relatively protective. Arizona's expanded ban reaches into club sports and Little League, and Nebraska's is now locked into its constitution. For trans young people in those states, the message of the ballot was not ambiguous, and it hurt.

On the other side, the same election put repeal on the table in four states where it was not on the table before — including Arizona, where a care ban could be removed by a legislature for the first time anywhere in the country, and Missouri, where a ban is already scheduled to expire. Add in the trans lawmakers who won on Tuesday, the governors who opposed these measures, and the advocacy groups pledging legal challenges, and the picture is not a rout. It is a fight — and one where both sides won ground.

Here is what we would say to you, loves, if you are reading this from Colorado, Arizona, Nebraska, or Washington and feeling like the ground shifted under you: you are not imagining it, and you are not alone in it. The organizations that fight these measures in court and in legislatures are already moving. The laws do not take effect until January 1 in Colorado, and challenges are coming. If you are a parent of a trans young person, or a young person yourself, the most practical thing you can do right now is get clear, local information — from your provider, from your school district, from the advocacy groups in your state — about what changes for you, specifically, and when. General panic helps no one. Specific knowledge does.

And if you are reading this from Arizona, Missouri, North Carolina, or Texas, pay attention to your 2027 legislative session. The repeal fights there will be decided by who shows up, who calls their representatives, and who refuses to let a new majority quietly sit on the votes it just won. Elections open doors. They do not walk through them for you — but for the first time in a long while, in those four states, the door is actually open.

What you can do next: If you are in Colorado, mark January 1, 2027 — that is when Propositions 134 and 135 take effect. Surgeries for minors are still legal until then, and puberty blockers and hormones are not affected by Proposition 135 at all. Follow One Colorado for updates on legal challenges. If you are in Arizona, Missouri, North Carolina, or Texas, watch your 2027 legislative session: the repeal fights there will need public pressure to happen.
