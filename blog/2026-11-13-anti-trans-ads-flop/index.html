The $400 Million Flop: Voters Saw Through the Anti-Trans Ads — and Transgender Awareness Week Starts Today

Transgender Awareness Week begins today, November 13 — and it opens with a remarkable piece of news. The most expensive anti-trans advertising campaign in American history just failed, in public, on the biggest stage there is. More than $400 million was spent telling voters to be afraid of trans people. Voters were not afraid. They were annoyed.

November 13, 2026 · 6 min read

The number that tells the story

Loves, start with the money, because the money is the story. The Human Rights Campaign, tabulating data from the ad-tracking firm AdImpact, put total spending on anti-trans advertising in the 2026 election cycle at more than $400 million — about $240 million on broadcast television, $100 million on cable, and more than $50 million online. A separate Associated Press analysis counted an estimated $183 million spent on transgender-focused ads in statewide and federal races alone, most of it attacking Democratic candidates for supporting trans people. The two figures cover different scopes, but they point the same way: this was not a side issue for Republican campaigns. It was the strategy.

And the strategy lost. "Anti-trans politics was a flop this election," Rep. Sarah McBride of Delaware — the first transgender member of Congress — said on a press call with the Human Rights Campaign on Monday. "They were a flop in 2025." Human Rights Campaign President Kelley Robinson was blunter about the intent: "The other side bet on $400 million of anti-trans ads. It was an effort to distract voters from the affordability crisis, and it backfired."

Even Florida Gov. Ron DeSantis, who has signed some of the most extreme anti-LGBTQ+ laws in the country, said trans issues were "not the issue motivating voters" and called the focus on them a failed attempt to "scare Republicans to vote" — "yesterday's battle," in his words. When the governor who built his national brand on this issue says it did not move voters, that is not spin. That is a concession.

What voters actually said

The polling behind the headlines is striking, and it comes from a survey conducted on the eve of the election by Greenberg Quinlan Rosner, a political polling firm, of 750 voters nationwide. Its findings were consistent with exit polling, according to the firm's senior partner Anna Greenberg.

Nearly half of voters — 47% — said they saw ads attacking Democrats for "allowing people who were born as men or transgender people to compete in women's sports" or "allowing gender changing surgery on children." The ads reached people. They just did not persuade them. When asked whether they wanted to hear more, less, or the same amount about trans issues during the election, 65% of voters said they wanted to hear less, and only 14% wanted to hear more. By comparison, 62% said they wished they had heard more about the cost of living.

Asked whether the Republicans' "obsession with transgender people and children is strange and over the top," 58% of voters agreed — a larger share than voted for Democrats in Congress. That included 61% of young men and 62% of independent voters, two groups the ads were plainly aimed at. And when Republican voters were asked why they supported Republican candidates, only 3% mentioned trans issues at all. Two percent mentioned trans people in women's sports. One percent mentioned gender-affirming surgery for children.

An October poll from Navigator Research tells the same story from a different angle: voters from both parties ranked transgender issues last out of 20 issues, behind inflation, jobs, healthcare, housing, and immigration. The ads were loud. The voters had other things on their minds — and they resented being told otherwise.

The candidates the ads targeted mostly won

The ads were not abstract. They named names, and the names mostly won anyway. Ohio Sen.-elect Sherrod Brown, Michigan Sen.-elect Abdul El-Sayed, Texas Gov.-elect Gina Hinojosa, and Texas Sen.-elect James Talarico were all targeted by trans-focused attack ads. All four won their elections.

The Texas Senate race was the most extreme example. Republican political action committees spent at least $78 million on ads attacking Talarico's support of trans people — nearly half of his opponent's total ad spending. One ad said Talarico was "for they/them, not you," echoing language used against Kamala Harris in the 2024 presidential election. Another falsely claimed he supported jailing parents who would not allow their children to physically transition. He won anyway.

Trans candidates themselves had a strong night, too. Alabama, Georgia, Michigan, Washington, and Wisconsin elected their first transgender representatives to statewide office, and about a dozen other trans candidates were reelected to state-level positions. Jaelynn Scott became Washington's first out trans state legislator — and the first Black trans woman elected to any state legislature in the country. We covered the broader rainbow wave on November 6; the post-election analysis only sharpens the point. The people who know trans people, and the voters who met trans candidates, were on our side.

The honest other side

Loves, we will not pretend this election was a clean win, because it was not — and the same week that produced these numbers also produced real harm. Voters in Arizona, Colorado, Nebraska, and Washington approved anti-trans ballot measures, including bans on trans students playing school sports and, in Colorado, a ban on gender-affirming surgery for minors. We covered Colorado's measures on November 7 and Washington's Initiative 638 — and the lawsuit filed against it on November 9 — on November 11. The pushback there has already begun, but the measures passed, and that matters.

There is a second uncomfortable truth. It was not only Republicans who used trans lives as a campaign prop. Democrats in at least 17 states took anti-trans positions to try to win votes. Ohio Rep. Marcy Kaptur ran a reelection ad saying she had "voted with Republicans to keep transgender ideology out of our schools." Ohio Gov.-elect Amy Acton said she does "not support boys playing in girls' sports." The Human Rights Campaign declined to endorse either of them over those stances, and a former Ohio Democratic Party Pride Caucus vice chair resigned in protest after the party kept supporting Acton.

"Putting a D behind your name will no longer serve as a signal to us that you back us," former council member Arienne Childrey told Prism Reports. "You've got to win us." That is the lesson of this election in one sentence: the voters rejected the politics of cruelty, but the community is done accepting support that comes with conditions. The work ahead is not only defeating anti-trans attacks. It is holding every elected official — of either party — to the standard that trans rights are human rights, not a bargaining chip.

Why this is the right way to open Transgender Awareness Week

Transgender Awareness Week runs every year from November 13 through November 19, leading into Transgender Day of Remembrance on November 20. The week exists to increase understanding of trans people and the issues the community faces, and the day of remembrance — founded by transgender advocate Gwendolyn Ann Smith in 1999 to honor Rita Hester, a trans woman killed the previous year — honors trans people whose lives were lost to anti-trans violence.

This year, the week opens with proof that the visibility it calls for actually works. A letter from Gender Justice League, a Seattle trans advocacy group, put it plainly after Washington voters passed Initiative 638 on the same night they elected Jaelynn Scott: "The people who know us are on our side. The problem is: most Washingtonians have never knowingly met a trans person." That is exactly what this week is for.

And the stakes of the week ahead are concrete. The Human Rights Campaign says the biggest priorities for the new Congress include passing the Equality Act, restoring funding for HIV care and research, housing, and Medicaid ahead of a December 11 funding deadline, and holding the administration accountable — including Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, who has prioritized removing trans service members from the military. LGBTQ+ voters are 12% of the electorate today, and estimates project they will be 20% — one in five voters — by 2040. The $400 million flop did not end the fight. But it proved something the community has been saying for years: when voters actually see trans people, rather than caricatures of them, the fear stops working.

That is what this week is about, loves. Not awareness for its own sake — awareness that changes what happens next.

What you can do this week: If you are trans and it is safe for you to do so, tell your story — to a neighbor, a coworker, a family member. If you are an ally, say out loud that you love a trans person and what that means to you. Attend a local Transgender Awareness Week event or a Transgender Day of Remembrance vigil on November 20.
