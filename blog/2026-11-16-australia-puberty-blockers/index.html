Australia's Top Health Body Just Backed Puberty Blockers for Trans Youth — and Two States Said No Anyway

On Monday, November 16, 2026, Australia's National Health and Medical Research Council released the country's first nationally endorsed clinical advice on puberty blockers — and it says they should remain an option for some trans young people. Within hours, two state governments said they will keep their bans anyway.

November 16, 2026 · 8 min read

What happened today

Loves, this one is worth understanding properly, because it is the kind of story that will get flattened into a single headline — "Australia approves puberty blockers" or "Australia rejects puberty blockers" — and neither version is the whole truth. So here is the whole truth, as carefully as we can tell it.

Australia's National Health and Medical Research Council, the NHMRC, published interim advice today titled Advice on the use of puberty suppressants in the care of trans and gender diverse people aged under 18. Puberty suppressants — usually called puberty blockers — are medications that pause the production of estrogen or testosterone, delaying the onset or progression of puberty. The NHMRC's advice is that they can be considered as a treatment option for some young people under 18 who experience gender incongruence, under specific conditions and with strict safeguards.

This is the first time Australia has had nationally endorsed clinical advice on puberty blockers. The guidance that clinics had been working from before this dated to 2018, and it was never endorsed by the NHMRC. That matters: in a debate where everyone claims to speak for "the evidence," the country's own peak medical research body has now gone through the evidence, in public, and said what it actually supports.

The advice is now open for public consultation until 1 pm AEDT on January 18, 2027. It is interim — the full national guidelines on the care of trans and gender diverse young people, covering gender-affirming hormones and psychosocial support as well, are expected to go out for consultation in early 2028 and to be published in late 2028.

What the guidance actually says

The NHMRC's recommendation is a conditional one, and the conditions are specific. Puberty blockers may be considered for a young person under 18 who has gender incongruence — a persistent mismatch between the gender they experience and the sex recorded for them at birth — once they have begun puberty, once they have been assessed as suitable for treatment, and once the decision is reached through shared decision-making involving the young person, their parents or carers, and their clinicians.

Federal Health Minister Mark Butler, who asked the NHMRC to do this review last year, described the safeguards the same way in a press conference today: decisions "should be informed by the provision of appropriate information to the young person and their parents or carers, a comprehensive health assessment, multidisciplinary input tailored by a healthcare team to the needs of the young person, and regular monitoring."

The NHMRC's acting chief executive, Prue Torrance, put the point in human terms: "Young people and their families should be able to have informed discussions with their healthcare professionals, supported by the best available evidence and clear, trustworthy clinical guidance. Puberty suppressants are one possible part of care, and decisions about their use need to be made through shared decision making that involves the individual young person, their parents or carers and clinicians."

Two other parts of the advice deserve to be named plainly, because they cut against the loudest talking points in this debate. First, the council affirmed that gender incongruence is not a mental disorder, and it removed any requirement for a young person to demonstrate active distress — a diagnosis of gender dysphoria — before they can access treatment. Second, it explicitly rejected the "wait and see" approach pushed by opponents of gender-affirming care, describing it as a form of withholding care that "can affect the young person's physical and mental health, wellbeing, and ability to function."

That second point is a big one. "Wait and see" sounds gentle and cautious. The NHMRC is saying, after reviewing the evidence, that doing nothing is not a neutral option — it is itself a decision with consequences for a young person's health.

The honest part: what the evidence does and doesn't show

Here is the part a trustworthy blog has to include, even though it complicates the good news: the NHMRC judged the overall evidence on the benefits and risks of puberty blockers to be of low certainty. The recommendation rests mainly on 15 short-term, non-randomised observational studies. The council found that treatment may improve psychosocial functioning and might reduce suicidal behaviours — but it found no eligible evidence on effects on fertility or on long-term bone health.

That is why the recommendation is conditional rather than strong, and why it is framed as a decision to be made case by case, with a young person, their family, and their clinical team — not a blanket rule in either direction. Low-certainty evidence is not the same as no evidence, and it is not the same as evidence of harm. It means the honest answer right now is: the available evidence points toward benefit for some young people, the unknowns are real, and the decision belongs with the people who know the individual young person, not with politicians or pundits.

It is worth putting this in the context of the other evidence we have covered here. Just last week, we wrote about the largest study yet on hormone therapy in young people, published November 9 in JAMA Pediatrics: of 376 people who started hormone therapy as teenagers, 96.8% said it was the right decision, and only 2.7% reported regret. That study is about hormones, not blockers, and the two treatments are not the same thing — blockers pause puberty and are generally described as reversible, while hormones bring on physical changes. But the pattern of the evidence keeps pointing in the same direction: when young people, their families, and their clinicians make these decisions together, the overwhelming majority are glad they did.

Scale matters here, too. At his press conference today, Butler noted that the big specialist services, like the one in Melbourne, have prescribed puberty blockers to "maybe several dozen young people over the course of several years." This is not a treatment being handed out casually. It is a small, carefully assessed group of young people, in specialist clinics, over years.

Why this review happened at all

The review was not an academic exercise. It was commissioned because two Australian jurisdictions — Queensland and the Northern Territory — imposed bans on puberty blockers and gender-affirming hormones in their public hospitals. Those bans were what prompted Butler, in early 2025, to ask the NHMRC to review the evidence and develop national guidelines. Work on the guidelines began in February 2025, and the puberty blocker advice was prioritised for early release.

The debate had also been sharpened by the Cass Review in the United Kingdom, delivered in 2024, which led to tightened restrictions there and a wave of public commentary in Australia. Butler acknowledged that today, while also calling out the tone of some of that commentary: "Not all of that public commentary and debate is always constructive, and frankly sometimes it runs the risk of being quite damaging, particularly damaging to the mental health of those young Australians and their families."

So the NHMRC's advice lands in a country where the national health body and two state governments now point in opposite directions — and the question is what happens next.

Two states said no within hours

Queensland's health minister, Tim Nicholls, rejected the new guidance, saying the state government had made its own decision based on its own independent review. Queensland's restrictions on starting young people on puberty blockers or gender-affirming hormones in public hospitals will continue until 2031, when the UK's PATHWAYS clinical trial is due to conclude. The Northern Territory has likewise said it will keep its public-hospital restrictions in place.

Butler said the federal government cannot force the states to lift their bans, and that it will not intervene in existing state laws. So here is the honest, frustrating picture: the national body says this care can be appropriate for some young people, under strict safeguards — and in two jurisdictions, those young people still cannot get it through the public system. Puberty blockers remain legal across Australia and can be prescribed by clinicians, but a ban in public hospitals still decides, in practice, who can actually access care — because for most families, the public system is the only affordable route.

That split is the story to watch. The NHMRC's advice is not enforceable on the states, but it puts real pressure on them. It is much harder for a government to keep a ban in place when the country's own peak medical research body has reviewed the evidence and said the treatment should remain an option. The consultation period runs until January 18, 2027 — and the voices of young people, families, and clinicians in that consultation will help decide whether the interim advice becomes final guidance, and whether the states that said no today feel the ground shifting under them.

Why this matters from here

Loves, we cover stories like this from the United States most days — more than two dozen states here restrict this same care for young people, and the federal government has been moving to cut off funding for it. So it is easy to forget that this fight is global, and that the pattern is the same everywhere: the evidence gets reviewed by the people whose job is to review evidence, and the people who disagree with the evidence try to ban the care anyway.

Today's news is a win, and it is worth saying so plainly: the peak medical research body of an entire country looked at the evidence on puberty blockers and said they should stay on the table for the young people who need them, decided together with their families and their doctors. That is what evidence-based care looks like. It is not a slogan. It is a committee reading fifteen studies, weighing what they show and what they don't, and writing down, in public, that a young person's care should be decided by that young person, their family, and their clinician.

But it is also a reminder, on day four of Transgender Awareness Week, that a good guideline is not the same thing as access. A young person in Queensland still cannot start this treatment in a public hospital, no matter what the NHMRC says. Guidelines open doors. They do not walk through them for you — and in Queensland and the Northern Territory, the doors are still shut, with a date of 2031 written on them.

So here is the part that belongs to all of us: the consultation is open until January 18, 2027. If you are in Australia — a young person, a parent, a clinician, or an ally — that is a real chance to be heard by the body writing the rules. And if you are reading this from the United States, or anywhere else, the lesson travels: when the evidence is on our side, the fight becomes about making sure the people in power cannot simply ignore it. Australia's health body did its job today. Now the states have to answer for theirs.
