The Rainbow Wave: Trans Candidates Won Big on Tuesday — and Missouri Said No to a Care Ban

Tuesday, November 3, 2026, was a night of firsts: at least eleven trans and nonbinary candidates won their races across the country, and Missouri voters rejected a ballot measure that would have written a ban on gender-affirming care for minors into the state constitution.

November 6, 2026 · 5 min read

A wave, not a ripple

Loves, let's start with the headline number, because it deserves to be said plainly: at least eleven trans and nonbinary candidates won on Tuesday. That count, confirmed by Pride Source's election-night reporting, covers first-time winners and incumbents who held their seats — people running in states from Washington to Florida, in big cities and small districts, in races for Congress, state houses, and local boards. It is the kind of night that would have been hard to imagine even a few years ago, and it happened in an election cycle where trans people were the subject of attack ads and ballot measures in state after state.

That context matters. These wins did not happen in a quiet year. They happened while voters were also being asked to decide ballot measures about trans kids in sports and trans people's medical care. The candidates who won did so with their identities known, their names on the ballot, and their communities watching. Voters looked at them and said yes anyway — in some of the most conservative states in the country.

Representation is not a magic wand, and we will get to the honest part of that in a minute. But it is not nothing, either. When the people writing health policy have lived the experience of seeking that care, the conversation in the room changes. A legislator who has waited months for an appointment, fought an insurance denial, or driven hours to reach an affirming provider does not need a briefing paper to understand what is at stake. They already know.

The firsts, one by one

Five states elected their first trans state lawmakers on Tuesday, and Florida elected its first out trans person to any public office. Here is who made history, and where:

  • Jaelynn Scott — Washington. Scott won a seat in the Washington House of Representatives, becoming the state's first openly trans lawmaker and the first out Black trans woman elected to any state legislature in the country. She leads the Lavender Rights Project, a Black- and trans-led advocacy organization, and campaigned on defending gender-affirming care from federal attacks.
  • Bentley Hudgins — Georgia. Hudgins, who uses they/them pronouns, won a seat in the Georgia House, becoming the first out trans and nonbinary person elected to the Georgia General Assembly. They previously served as the Human Rights Campaign's Georgia state director.
  • Joanna Whaley — Michigan. Whaley won Michigan's 2nd House District, becoming the first openly trans person elected to the Michigan Legislature.
  • Dina Nina Martinez-Rutherford — Wisconsin. Martinez-Rutherford won a seat in the Wisconsin Assembly, becoming the state's first openly transgender legislator.
  • Allison Montgomery — Alabama. Montgomery won a seat in the Alabama House, becoming the state's first openly transgender state legislator — a first in a state where trans rights have been under sustained legislative attack.
  • Quinnie Pérez — Florida. Pérez won Seat 1 on the Orange County Soil and Water Conservation District board, becoming the first out transgender woman elected to public office in Florida. The board is a small, nonpartisan, volunteer body — but in a state whose leaders have spent years restricting trans people's rights, the symbolism of the win is hard to overstate.

Alongside the first-timers, trans incumbents held their ground. Sarah McBride was re-elected to the U.S. House from Delaware, Zooey Zephyr was re-elected in Montana, and Leigh Finke was re-elected in Minnesota. Holding a seat matters just as much as winning a new one: it means voters had a chance to judge a trans lawmaker on their record and chose to send them back.

Texas made history, too

There was one more result worth naming, because it matters for trans health in the state with the country's largest trans population under a care ban. Democrat Gina Hinojosa defeated Republican Governor Greg Abbott in Texas, becoming the first Latina governor in Texas history. Hinojosa, a state representative from Austin, had voted against SB 14, the 2023 Texas law that banned gender-affirming care for minors. Her win does not by itself repeal that law — the Texas Legislature still has to act, and the governor's office is one seat at a crowded table — but it puts someone who opposed the ban in the state's highest office for the first time since the ban was written.

Missouri said no

Now to the other headline of the night, and the one that speaks most directly to trans health: Missouri voters rejected Amendment 3, a ballot measure that would have banned gender-affirming care for minors in the state constitution. The "No" side won with about 55 percent of the vote, according to unofficial results reported on election night.

Here is what a "No" vote actually meant, in plain language. Amendment 3 was a package deal: it would have repealed the abortion protections Missouri voters adopted in 2024 and replaced them with a near-total abortion ban, and it would have written a ban on gender-affirming care for minors — surgeries, puberty blockers, and hormone therapy — into the state constitution. Voting "No" meant rejecting all of it. Voting "Yes" would have put both the abortion ban and the care ban into the constitution, where they would have been far harder to undo than an ordinary law.

It is important to be honest about what the "No" vote did and did not do. It did not repeal Missouri's existing restrictions on care for minors. Those were passed by the legislature in 2023 and they are still in effect: the ban on surgeries for minors has no expiration date, and the limits on puberty blockers and hormone therapy are set to expire in August 2027 unless lawmakers act. What the "No" vote did was stop those restrictions from being locked into the constitution without an expiration date. Because the amendment failed, there is now a real end date on the table for the limits on blockers and hormones — and a real fight ahead in the 2027 legislative session over whether lawmakers renew them.

There is another detail worth sitting with. Missouri is a state that voted for Donald Trump by 18 points in 2024, and its voters still rejected this measure by double digits. The campaign backing the amendment ran ads focused heavily on banning trans care for minors. Voters heard that message and voted no anyway. That is a signal worth noticing: even in a deeply conservative state, a majority of voters were not willing to write a care ban into their constitution.

What this means for trans health access

So what do these two results — a wave of trans winners and a popular vote against a care ban — actually mean for trans health? The honest answer is: a lot, and not everything. Let's take both halves seriously.

First, the hopeful half. More trans voices in the rooms where health policy gets made is a genuine change in how care gets decided. State legislators vote on Medicaid coverage, insurance rules, clinic funding, and the laws that decide whether a provider can offer care at all. A legislature that includes trans people is a legislature where someone can say, from lived experience, what a waiting list or a denial letter actually costs a person. That changes debates, and over time it changes votes. And the Missouri result shows that care bans are not automatically popular, even where you might expect them to be. When voters are asked directly, in plain language, whether to ban care for trans kids, a majority in Missouri said no. That is evidence organizers and advocates can carry into the next fight.

Now the grounded half, because hope without honesty does not help anyone. One election does not fix access. If you are trans and trying to get care today, the barriers you face are still real: appointments that cost more than you can afford, insurance plans that deny coverage, too few affirming providers in too many places, and long drives or long waits to reach the ones that exist. A state-by-state patchwork still decides whether the same care is legal, covered, or available depending on which side of a state line you live on. Missouri's vote did not restore care for minors in Missouri — the 2023 restrictions are still law. Texas still has its ban on care for minors, and one governor cannot erase it alone. And the new lawmakers who won on Tuesday will take office in January into legislatures where they will often be in the minority, arguing for care in rooms that have spent years voting against it.

None of that takes away from what happened on Tuesday. It just puts it in its proper place: a beginning, not an ending. Elections open doors. They do not walk through them for you. The work of turning representation into access — funding clinics, protecting coverage, training providers, keeping bans from coming back — is still ahead, and it will take the same steady organizing that won these races in the first place.

If you are reading this and feeling a little lighter than you did on Monday, let yourself feel that. Joy is not naive. It is fuel. And if you are reading this from a state where the news was harder, or where care still feels out of reach, you are not forgotten. The wave reached Alabama and Georgia and Florida this week. The map is changing, slowly and unevenly — but it is changing.
