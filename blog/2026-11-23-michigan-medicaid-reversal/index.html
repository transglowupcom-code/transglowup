Michigan Changed Its Mind: Trans Kids on Medicaid Keep Their Care — and the State Is Footing the Bill

Michigan was on the list of states that planned to end Medicaid coverage of gender-affirming care for trans minors. Then, quietly, it changed course: the state will keep covering the care and pay for it with its own money instead of claiming federal dollars. For the families who were bracing to lose coverage, that reversal is the difference between care and no care.

November 23, 2026 · 7 min read

The reversal, in plain language

Loves, here is what happened. On November 2, the Michigan Department of Health and Human Services — MDHHS, the agency that runs Michigan's Medicaid program — issued a bulletin, MMP 26-42, to every Medicaid provider and health plan in the state. The bulletin said Michigan will stop claiming federal matching funds for puberty blockers and surgery provided to minors for gender affirmation. That part is Michigan complying with the federal rule. But the bulletin did not end the coverage. Instead, the state said it will keep the care going and pay for it itself: according to the state's own announcement, "the State of Michigan Medicaid Program will continue to cover these treatments according to Michigan Medicaid fee-for-service pharmacy rules and restrictions using state general funds."

Read that again, because the distinction is the whole story. The federal government said: we will not pay for this care. Michigan answered: fine, we will. The money changes hands, not the medicine. Existing approvals stay good, too — the bulletin says MDHHS "will honor [Medicaid Health Plans]-approved prior authorizations (PA) for affected services occurring on and after October 13, 2026," the date the federal rule took effect. If your child's care was already approved, that approval still counts.

This is a real reversal. In September, MDHHS had signaled the opposite. On September 9, the agency sent a letter — to only about 30 recipients, all tribal chairs or health directors for Native American communities — saying it would amend Michigan's Medicaid State Plan to adhere to the federal prohibition on claiming federal and CHIP funds for gender-affirming procedures for minors. Reporting at the time put Michigan on the list of states ending coverage, alongside Arizona, Virginia, and Colorado. Then on November 2, the state published a different answer.

How we got here: the federal rule that started it

To understand why Michigan had a choice to make at all, you have to go back to August. The Trump administration finalized a rule — CMS-2451-F — barring the use of federal Medicaid and CHIP funds for gender-affirming care for young people: under 18 in Medicaid, under 19 in CHIP. The rule took effect on October 13, 2026. We covered it here on November 8: it affects an estimated 130,000 trans youth, and it set a six-month countdown running for kids already on hormone therapy, with federal funding for those prescriptions tapering off around mid-April 2027.

But here is the thing the rule's authors either missed or counted on states missing: Medicaid and CHIP are jointly funded. Washington pays part, the state pays part. The rule only blocks the federal share. Nothing in it forces a state to stop covering the care — a state can keep the coverage in place by paying for that specific care entirely with its own money and simply not claiming the federal match for it. That is the door Michigan just walked through.

And Michigan is not the first. Pennsylvania made the same move in mid-October, announcing it would use state funds to protect trans kids' access. With Michigan, PinkNews reports that 16 states now have confirmed policies that will help preserve funding for gender-affirming care for trans minors on Medicaid and CHIP. A coalition of 21 states plus Washington, D.C., has also sued to challenge the federal rule itself — that lawsuit was filed on September 2, and it is still working its way through the courts.

What actually changes for Michigan families

Good news deserves specifics, so here is what the bulletin actually does — and what it does not do.

First, the mechanics. Gender-affirmation services are carved out of Michigan's Medicaid health plans for beneficiaries of all ages, effective October 13, 2026. That means the care is no longer routed through the managed-care plans; it runs through the state's fee-for-service system instead. New prior-authorization requests for affected services on or after October 13 must go directly to MDHHS's Program Review Division, not to a health plan. If a family needs to modify an existing authorization, they submit medical documentation to that same division. Mental health services delivered through the state's Prepaid Inpatient Health Plans are not affected by the change at all. And the bulletin was issued while public comment is being accepted at the same time — families and providers can still weigh in.

Second, the hormone therapy clock. The bulletin includes a narrow, time-limited exception: gender-affirming hormone therapy can still draw federal matching funds through April 13, 2027. That lines up with the six-month taper we wrote about on November 8. After that date, federal dollars for youth hormone therapy end, and the question becomes whether Michigan's state-funded coverage picks up hormones too. The bulletin does not spell that out in so many words, and that is a question Michigan families and their doctors should be asking the state directly, now, not in April.

Third, the honest limits. The reporting describes Michigan as continuing "some" gender-affirming care, and that word matters. The bulletin does not publish a dollar figure, and it does not print a neat list of every service that stays covered. What it does say, plainly, is that the state will keep covering these treatments under its fee-for-service rules using state general funds, and that approved authorizations will be honored. That is a commitment, but it is a commitment that lives in a state budget and a state bulletin — which means a future legislature or a future administration could revisit it. Coverage that depends on a state's willingness to pay is real coverage, and it is also coverage that needs defending.

The map is splitting in two

Michigan's reversal does not happen in a vacuum. The country is splitting into two maps, and the line between them runs right through Medicaid offices in state capitals.

On one side, the states paying their own way: Michigan and Pennsylvania, plus the others PinkNews counts among the 16 with confirmed policies to preserve funding. On the other side, the states that went along with the cut. Connecticut and Rhode Island are the latest to capitulate, ending Medicaid coverage for trans youth in response to the federal rule. Arizona, Virginia, and Colorado made the same choice earlier. And underneath all of it, the Movement Advancement Project counts 26 states with laws banning medically necessary medication and surgical care for transgender youth outright — bans no Medicaid workaround can fix, because the care itself is illegal there, not just unfunded.

So the picture is this: in about half the country, a trans kid's access to care now depends on which state they live in and whether that state's government is willing to spend its own money. That is not a fair system. It is the system the federal rule created, and Michigan just showed that states do not have to accept the cruelest version of it. Every state that chooses the state-funds route makes it harder for the next state to claim it had no choice.

What this means for trans health

Let us be clear about what Michigan did and did not do. It did not defeat the federal rule. It did not restore federal funding. It did not help a single trans kid in Texas or Florida or any of the 26 states with bans. What it did was refuse to let a federal funding rule become a state coverage cut — and for the trans young people on Michigan Medicaid and CHIP, that refusal is everything. Their blockers, their surgeries, their approved authorizations: still covered. The state just pays the bill now instead of splitting it with Washington.

There is a lesson in this one for the rest of us, too. When the federal rule landed in August, the first wave of coverage treated it as a done deal — the money was gone, the care was gone. But the rule was never a ban on care. It was a ban on federal dollars for care, and the states were always the ones who decided what happened next. Some states chose to cut. Michigan chose to pay. The difference between those two choices is a governor, a legislature, a health department, and a public that makes its voice heard. That means the fight over this rule is not only in federal court, where 21 states and D.C. are challenging it. It is also in every state capital, in every Medicaid bulletin, in every budget line — and it is winnable there, one state at a time.

If you are a parent in Michigan reading this, here is the practical part: if your child's care was approved by a Medicaid health plan, that approval stands for services on or after October 13, 2026. New requests go to MDHHS's Program Review Division now, not to the health plan — so if a request stalls, that is the office to call. And if your child is on hormone therapy, mark April 13, 2027, on the calendar and ask your provider and the state now what happens to that coverage when the federal funding window closes. Do not wait for spring to find out.

And if you are reading this from a state that cut coverage, you are not forgotten. Michigan was on the cut list, too — until it was not. States change their minds. Pressure works. The map is still being drawn, and this week, it moved in our direction.

What to watch next: The 21-state lawsuit challenging the federal rule is still pending — watch for any ruling that could restore federal funding outright. In Michigan, watch whether the state confirms hormone therapy stays covered with state funds after the April 13, 2027 federal funding deadline. And in every state capital, watch the next Medicaid bulletin: Michigan proved the state-funds route works, and other states are deciding right now whether to follow it.
