Almost Nobody Regrets It: A New Study Asked 376 Young People About Hormone Therapy

A study published Monday in JAMA Pediatrics did something the loudest voices in the care debate almost never do: it asked the young people themselves. Of 376 people who started hormone therapy as teenagers, 96.8% said it was the right decision — and only 2.7% reported any regret. Here's what the numbers actually say, and why they land at exactly the moment they do.

November 12, 2026 · 6 min read

The headline numbers

Loves, here is the whole study in one paragraph. Researchers at the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center surveyed 376 transgender and gender-diverse people who had started estrogen or testosterone therapy as young people at a single multidisciplinary gender clinic in the United States. The participants are now 14 to 28 years old, with an average age of about 21, and on average they started hormone therapy nearly five years ago — some as long as 10.7 years ago. When asked, on a standard decision-regret questionnaire, whether starting hormones was the right decision, 360 of the 372 who answered — 96.8% — said yes. Ten people, 2.7%, reported regret. Seven people, 1.9%, said they felt harmed by the treatment. The average regret score across the whole group was 4.3 out of 100, and the median score was zero.

Those numbers are not a rounding error, and they are not a fluke of asking the wrong people. The study team deliberately recruited former patients even if they had stopped treatment, were no longer receiving care, or had moved away. And 65 of the participants — 17.3% — were not currently receiving hormone therapy when they answered. The researchers wanted the people most likely to be unhappy with their decision in the room, and the result barely moved.

The study's authors put the meaning plainly: the results "provide support for existing standards of care rather than broad restrictions on youths' access to hormone therapy." That is the sentence the bans were never built to survive.

How the study actually worked

It is worth understanding what this study did, because the details are what make it hard to dismiss. The participants were former patients of a US multidisciplinary program for trans youth. Starting hormone therapy there followed the World Professional Association for Transgender Health's standards of care and the Endocrine Society's guidelines — including a structured assessment with a mental health professional, and, for patients under 18, consent from every parent or guardian with medical decision-making rights, on top of the young person's own assent.

The surveys were collected between October 2023 and October 2025, and the analysis ran from December 2025 to August 2026. The work was funded by the National Institutes of Health, through the Eunice Kennedy Shriver National Institute of Child Health and Human Development. Participants answered the Decision Regret Scale, a five-question measure that asks people to reflect on their decision to start estrogen or testosterone therapy, and they were also asked directly whether they felt harmed by it. Starting in October 2024, anyone who said they regretted the decision or felt harmed was invited to describe the experience in their own words.

Of the 376 participants, 112 started estrogen and 264 started testosterone. Nearly 92% had started hormones at age 17 or younger. This was not a group of people asked a week after their first dose — the average time since starting was 4.9 years.

And the study is honest about its limits, which is part of why it is worth trusting. It comes from a single clinic, so it cannot speak for every program in the country. It relies on self-reported surveys. And not everyone the team tried to reach answered — participants assigned male at birth, and those who had experienced physical dysphoria before puberty, were less likely to complete the survey. Those are real caveats. They do not change the direction of the finding, but they are the reason one study, however strong, is a piece of evidence rather than the whole case.

The small numbers matter too — so let's look at them honestly

A careful study does not hide the people who had a bad experience, and neither will we. Ten people reported regret, and seven said they felt harmed. Regret scores ran slightly higher among two groups: people who were no longer receiving hormone therapy, and people who identify on the nonbinary spectrum. Even in those groups, the researchers stress, regret remained low in absolute terms — a difference of degrees, not a different story.

That pattern points to something the study's authors spell out clearly: stopping treatment is not the same thing as regretting it. People stop hormones for many reasons — insurance barriers, cost, moving, losing a supportive clinician, family pressure, or simply because their goals for treatment changed. The paper cites earlier research showing that only about 2% of young people who started puberty suppression as adolescents were no longer receiving hormone therapy a few years later, and that in another clinic sample, of the 57 people who paused treatment for at least three months, 20 later restarted. In the 2024 JAMA Pediatrics study of 220 young people we have written about before, nine reported regret — and four of them were still receiving care anyway. Regret and discontinuation overlap, but they are not the same thing, and treating them as identical is one of the oldest tricks in this debate.

There is also a finding that cuts against a common talking point. Neither regret, nor perceived harm, nor whether someone was still on hormones was associated with the age at which they started treatment. Starting younger did not predict more regret. That matters, because the whole architecture of the bans rests on the idea that younger patients are the ones being harmed by decisions made too early. The data do not show that.

Why this lands now

Loves, context is everything here. The fear of regret is the single most repeated justification for every restriction on youth care in this country. It is the argument written into state bans, the argument cited in court filings, and the argument behind the federal rule we covered on November 8 — the one that has barred Medicaid and CHIP funds from paying for gender-affirming care for trans youth since October 13, with a six-month countdown now running for kids already on hormones. As of September 2026, 27 states have enacted laws banning or severely limiting this care for minors. The claim, repeated endlessly, is that too many young people will come to regret it.

Now we have one of the most direct tests of that claim ever published, and it found the opposite. It joins a consistent body of work: more than a dozen short-term studies and several large follow-up studies have found improvements in mental health and quality of life among trans youth receiving hormone therapy, and the study's authors note that clinic-based studies of young people have documented regret rates between 0% and 2%. The 2024 Trans Youth Project study found 97% of young people still receiving care years later, with only about 4% expressing any regret. The new study's 96.8% sits right in that same range — different clinic, different sample, same answer.

None of this changes the law by itself. A study cannot repeal a statute, and the bans in 27 states will still need to be fought in legislatures and courtrooms — the same courtrooms where, just this week, Washington's new sports ban landed in a lawsuit and where the Supreme Court sent Darlene Griffith's jail case back for another look. But evidence does change what those fights are about. When a legislature claims it must ban care to protect children from regret, it is now arguing against peer-reviewed data collected from the very young people it claims to protect. That is a much harder argument to make in a courtroom than on a campaign ad.

What to watch next

Two things to keep an eye on. First, the legal fights. The Medicaid rule's six-month hormone taper for young people already on treatment runs to about mid-April 2027, and the 21-state lawsuit challenging that rule is still pending. Studies like this one are exactly the kind of evidence those cases will lean on — and the kind of evidence that makes a ban look like a solution in search of a problem. Second, the research itself. This study's authors note that stopping treatment is likely becoming more common as external barriers to care increase — insurance denials, clinic closures, state bans — which makes it more important, not less, to keep asking young people directly how they feel about the care they received, and to listen to the answer.

And one more date, loves: Transgender Awareness Week begins tomorrow, November 13, and runs through Transgender Day of Remembrance on November 20. If this study tells us anything, it is that the young people at the center of this debate know their own minds — and that the best thing the rest of us can do this week is believe them.

What you can do next: If someone in your life is weighing hormone therapy, share this study — the numbers are simple enough to carry into any conversation. If you are a parent, a teacher, or a clinician, remember that the young people in this study were assessed under established standards of care, with parental consent, and years later nearly all of them stand by the decision.
