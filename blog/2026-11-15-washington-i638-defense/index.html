Washington's Attorney General Won't Defend the Sports Ban Himself — So Who Will?

It is day three of Transgender Awareness Week, and the court fight over Washington's new ban on trans girls in school sports just got stranger. On Thursday, the state's attorney general announced that his own office will not defend the voter-approved measure — and hired an outside law firm to do it instead. On Friday, the ban's supporters went to court and asked to join the case themselves, saying they do not trust the state to defend it. With eighteen days left before the law takes effect, the question is no longer just whether the ban is constitutional. It is who, exactly, is going to stand up in court and argue for it.

November 15, 2026 · 6 min read

The attorney general steps aside

Loves, here is what happened on Thursday, November 12. Washington Attorney General Nick Brown announced that he is appointing the national law firm Bryan Cave Leighton Paisner — BCLP for short — to defend Initiative 638 on behalf of the state. The firm's lawyers will serve as Special Assistant Attorneys General, which is the formal title for outside counsel hired to represent the state when the Attorney General's Office cannot handle a case itself.

Brown's own statement explains why. His office, he said, has spent the last two years doing extensive work defending the rights of transgender Washingtonians — bringing and winning cases against the federal government to protect access to health care, and defending state laws that give trans people equal protection under Washington law. "We will not and cannot abandon that work," he wrote. And, he added, his personal views do not decide which laws get defended: "My personal views do not dictate what laws get defended or the strength of that defense."

There is also a concrete conflict sitting underneath the announcement. Brown's former law firm, Pacifica Law Group, is one of the firms representing the families challenging the initiative. Reporting on the announcement noted that conflict as one reason his office stepped back from defending the measure itself. Brown described appointing outside counsel as a common step when his office cannot handle a case — whether because of a conflict of interest, a need for specialized expertise, or workload — and called BCLP a well-respected national firm with a substantial presence in Washington state, whose lawyers have handled many important cases for the state before.

That is the official version, and it is worth taking seriously: an attorney general has a duty to defend laws the voters approved, and he is doing that — just not with his own lawyers.

The twist in the hire

But here is the part that made this story travel. The firm Brown hired is not a stranger to trans rights work. Seattle talk station 570 KVI reported that BCLP has represented the World Professional Association for Transgender Health — WPATH, the organization that writes the international standards of care for gender-affirming treatment — in litigation involving attorneys general in Montana, Idaho, Missouri, and Indiana, and that the firm has partnered with the ACLU to challenge restrictions on gender transitions for young people. A senior BCLP attorney has also publicly criticized sex-based sports restrictions, according to the station's reporting.

That does not mean the firm will do a bad job defending the initiative. Lawyers defend clients all the time whose positions they would not personally take — that is the job. But it is the kind of detail that tells you how tangled this case is. The state is defending a ban on trans girls in sports with a firm that has spent years on the other side of trans rights litigation, while the attorney general who hired them says his office will keep defending trans rights in every other case it has. Nobody in this story is pretending the politics are simple.

Brown's announcement did not name the specific BCLP attorneys assigned to the case or disclose what the defense will cost the state, according to the same reporting. Those are fair questions for a case the voters approved and the state is paying to defend.

Friday: the ban's supporters ask to join in

Then came Friday, November 13. The Alliance Defending Freedom, a conservative legal group, filed a motion asking King County Superior Court to let its client, Female Athletes United, join the case as a defendant — to help defend Initiative 638 alongside the state.

The group's argument is blunt: the state cannot be trusted to fully represent the athletes who support the ban. ADF attorney Hal Frampton told Fox News Digital that the group wants to step in "so that I-638 goes into effect on December 3rd, just like the voters in the state of Washington asked for." The motion points to Washington's prior defense of transgender participation in girls' sports as a reason the state might not advance every argument the group wants considered.

To be clear about what this filing is: it is a request, not a ruling. The court has not yet decided whether Female Athletes United gets to join the case. But the filing itself tells you something about the shape of this fight. The ban's supporters are not content to let the state handle the defense — even after the state hired outside lawyers specifically to do it. They want their own seat at the table.

A quick recap: what the lawsuit is actually about

If you missed our post on Wednesday, here is the story so far. On November 3, Washington voters approved Initiative 638 with roughly 56 percent of the vote. The measure generally bars trans girls from girls' interscholastic sports at public schools and at private schools that take part in covered athletics associations. Students entering designated girls' events would need a signed statement from their health care provider verifying their sex based on reproductive anatomy, genetic makeup, or naturally produced testosterone levels. The measure is scheduled to take effect December 3, 2026.

On Monday, November 9, parents, TransFamilies, and the Washington Education Association — the teachers' union, with more than 80,000 members — sued in King County Superior Court to block it before it takes effect. They are represented by the ACLU of Washington, Legal Voice, and Pacifica Law Group. Their argument is that the measure violates four provisions of the Washington state constitution, that it discriminates against trans girls, and that the sex-verification regime it creates breaches students' privacy — and applies to girls only. No boy has to prove his sex to play a sport. The plaintiffs are asking the court for an emergency order to stop the measure from taking effect on December 3. As of today, the court has not yet decided that request.

Seattle Public Schools' board voted the same day the lawsuit was filed to authorize legal action of its own. So the case now has families, a teachers' union, a major school district, the state, an outside law firm, and a national conservative legal group all circling the same courtroom — with eighteen days on the clock.

The honest other side

Loves, we will be straight with you about the hard parts of this story, because the details matter for the kids at the center of it.

First: the voters did approve this measure. Roughly 56 percent is not a squeaker, and the attorney general is right that a law the voters passed deserves a real defense in court. The families challenging it are not asking the court to ignore the election — they are asking the court to decide whether the measure violates the state constitution, which is exactly what courts are for. Both of those things can be true at once.

Second: whatever happens in court, the verification rules are the part of this law that will land hardest on real students, and not only on trans girls. If the law takes effect on December 3, any girl who wants to compete in a designated event will need a signed statement from a health care provider verifying her sex. That is a doctor's visit, a form, and a piece of paper about a child's body, just to play a school sport. The lawsuit argues that is a privacy violation, and it is worth sitting with that argument on its own terms — because it affects every girl on the team, not only the trans ones.

Third: the calendar is doing real damage even before any ruling. School sports seasons do not wait for court dates. Coaches, athletic directors, and families are trying to plan winter seasons right now without knowing whether the law will be in effect on December 3, whether the court will block it, or who will even be arguing for it when the hearing happens. Uncertainty is its own kind of harm, and it falls on the kids first.

Why this is the right story for day three of Transgender Awareness Week

Transgender Awareness Week runs November 13 through November 19, leading into Transgender Day of Remembrance on November 20. The week exists so more people can know trans people — and the evidence keeps saying that knowing trans people is what changes minds.

The Washington fight is, in a way, the whole week in miniature. A law that was sold as being about fairness in sports is being argued over by lawyers, law firms, and advocacy groups — while the actual trans girls it targets are mostly absent from the conversation, talked about rather than talked with. The families suing to block it include parents of trans girls and parents of cis girls, standing together, because the verification rules touch all of their daughters. That is the story underneath the legal maneuvering: ordinary families, on both sides of the question, trying to protect their kids.

And there is something quietly hopeful in the week's other headlines, too. On Friday, Chicago raised the Trans Pride flag for the first time at Logan Square Park to mark the start of the week — a ceremony hosted by the city's Advisory Council on LGBTQ Issues, co-sponsored by a city alderman, with trans leaders and allies speaking in the open air. The flag is still flying. The week is still going. And in Washington, the court has not decided anything yet — which means the outcome is still open, and the voices of trans kids and their families still have time to be heard.

What you can do this week: If you are a Washington family with a student athlete, watch the King County Superior Court case — the emergency ruling before December 3 is the one that matters most. Talk to your school's athletic director now about what the verification rules would require, so no student is caught off guard. Wherever you are, attend a Transgender Awareness Week event or a Transgender Day of Remembrance vigil on November 20.
