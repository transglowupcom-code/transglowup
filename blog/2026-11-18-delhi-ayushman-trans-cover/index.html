Delhi Just Gave 5,600 Trans People Free Health Coverage — But the Certificate That Unlocks It Is Harder to Get Than Ever

Yesterday, Delhi's cabinet approved up to ₹10 lakh a year in free, cashless health coverage for transgender residents and their families. It is a real win, and it is worth celebrating. It is also, loves, a win with a locked door in front of it — because the identity certificate that unlocks this coverage now requires a medical board's permission, after a law passed earlier this year erased self-identification from India's books.

November 18, 2026 · 8 min read

What Delhi actually approved

On Tuesday, November 17, 2026, the Delhi Cabinet, chaired by Chief Minister Rekha Gupta, approved the inclusion of eligible transgender people and their families under the Ayushman Bharat Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana, known as PM-JAY — India's national health assurance scheme. The decision was announced after the cabinet meeting at the Delhi Secretariat, and the Chief Minister's Office confirmed the details the same day.

Here is what the coverage means in practice. Eligible families will be entitled to cashless medical treatment worth up to ₹10 lakh per family per year — ₹5 lakh from the central government under PM-JAY, plus another ₹5 lakh contributed by the Delhi government. That covers 1,961 treatment packages across 27 medical specialities — cardiology, oncology, burns management, urology, ophthalmology, neurosurgery, ENT, and mental health care among them — at empanelled hospitals across the country, not just in Delhi.

The government's own estimate is that about 5,656 transgender people in Delhi will benefit, a figure worked out from the 2011 census, which counted 4,213 transgender residents in the capital. The Delhi government expects the expansion to cost about ₹1.19 crore a year from its budget. The move had been telegraphed months earlier: Delhi's 2026–27 budget set aside ₹202 crore to extend Ayushman Bharat coverage to transgender people, and the Centre and Delhi signed the agreement adding the extra ₹5 lakh top-up back in April 2025.

For a community that has historically faced locked doors at hospital reception desks — turned away, misgendered, or simply unable to pay — this is not a small thing. Free, cashless care at hospitals nationwide, including mental health treatment, is the kind of policy that changes whether someone actually goes to the doctor. Loves, that is what a health win looks like when a government decides your life is worth covering.

The catch: the certificate

Now the honest part, because a win you cannot reach is not a win yet. To qualify, a person must be on the revenue department's verified list and hold a valid transgender identity certificate issued by a district magistrate. That certificate used to be straightforward to get. It is not anymore.

Under India's original Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act of 2019, a transgender person could obtain an identity certificate through a simple affidavit before the district magistrate. The law's Section 4(2) stated plainly that a person recognised as transgender "shall have a right to self-perceived gender identity." That right was rooted in the Supreme Court's landmark 2014 NALSA v. Union of India judgment, which recognised self-identification as part of dignity, autonomy, and personal liberty under the Constitution.

The Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Amendment Act, 2026, took that away. The amendment was introduced in the Lok Sabha on March 13, 2026, passed by voice vote on March 24 amid an opposition walkout, affirmed by the Rajya Sabha the next day, signed into law on March 30, and came into force on May 25, 2026. It deletes Section 4(2) entirely. In its place, a district magistrate can only issue a certificate on the recommendation of a medical board headed by a chief medical officer or deputy chief medical officer — a board whose evidentiary standard is not defined in the law, with no clear time limit and no clearly accessible right of appeal.

The amendment also rewrote who counts as transgender at all. The old definition was broad and inclusive. The new one is a list: named socio-cultural identities such as hijra, kinner, eunuch, aravani, and jogta, plus people with specified intersex variations. Trans men, trans women, and genderqueer people are no longer in the statutory definition at all — and the law explicitly excludes people with "self-perceived sexual identities." The state, through a medical board and a district magistrate, now decides whether your identity is real enough to be recognised.

So here is the contradiction at the heart of yesterday's announcement. Delhi is offering free health care to transgender people — and the door to that care is a certificate that, since May, many trans people cannot get, because the law no longer recognises who they are.

The numbers show the gap is already real

This is not a theoretical problem. The Hindu's analysis, published this week, shows that transgender voters fell 31.4% — from 45,610 to 31,300 — in the states it analysed, and that Delhi's own draft voter rolls cut transgender electors by about 64%. When a community starts disappearing from official lists, that is not a clerical error. It is what happens when recognition becomes something you have to prove to a board instead of something you declare about yourself.

The certificate numbers tell the same story. Only 48,707 people have registered for transgender identity certificates — little more than 10% of the 4.8 lakh transgender people counted in the 2011 census — and more than 9,000 applications are still pending. Before the amendment, more than 32,000 certificates had been issued under the self-identification framework. The amendment did not just change a form. It narrowed the gate, and the people standing outside it are counted in the thousands.

That is why the Delhi announcement lands the way it does: genuinely good news, genuinely incomplete. The coverage is real, the money is real, and the 5,656 people the government expects to reach are real people who need care. But if the certificate gate keeps thousands more outside, the headline number will always be smaller than the need.

The fight is not over — it is in court

There is one more thing loves should know, because it is the reason this story is not finished. The 2026 amendment is being challenged before the Supreme Court of India. Petitions including Laxmi Narayan Tripathi v. Union of India argue that the amendment reverses what the Court itself declared a constitutional right in NALSA — that self-identification is part of dignity and personal liberty, not a privilege a medical board can grant or withhold. The Court heard the petitions on May 4, 2026, and the challenge is still pending.

The National Human Rights Commission has also weighed in this year, with a 2026 advisory identifying continuing discrimination and recommending self-identification, independent grievance-redressal mechanisms, and fewer documentation barriers — the opposite direction from the amendment.

None of that changes what happened yesterday. Delhi's cabinet did a good thing, and trans people in Delhi who hold the certificate can now get care they could not afford before. But the certificate itself is the fight now. Until the Court rules — or Parliament reverses course — the promise of free health care will reach the people the state recognises, and stop at the people it refuses to see.

Why this matters from here

Loves, it is day six of Transgender Awareness Week, and Transgender Day of Remembrance is two days away. This story is the week in miniature: a real, measurable gain — free health care, on paper, for thousands of trans people — sitting right next to a real, measurable erasure, as the law that decides who counts keeps narrowing.

That is the pattern of this whole week, and honestly of this whole year. The gains are real and they are worth naming: Australia's top health body backing puberty blockers on Monday, a federal appeals court hearing the defence of Colorado's Kelly Loving Act, Delhi opening its hospitals to trans families. And the erasures are real too, and they are worth naming just as plainly: a medical board standing between a person and their own name, voter rolls quietly shrinking, certificates pending in the thousands.

Both things are true at once. The work of this week is holding both of them — celebrating the doors that opened, and refusing to look away from the ones that are still locked. On Friday, we will say the names of the trans people we lost this year. Today, the least we can do is say clearly what the living still need: care they can actually reach, and a law that believes them when they say who they are.

What you can do next: If you are in Delhi and hold a transgender identity certificate, check your name on the National Health Authority's beneficiary portal at beneficiary.nha.gov.in to confirm your PM-JAY enrolment. If you are still waiting on a certificate, keep your application records and any correspondence — they matter if the Supreme Court challenge succeeds. And wherever you live, share this story: most people have never heard of India's 2026 amendment, and knowing that self-identification was erased by law is the first step to getting it back.
