Washington's Trans Sports Ban Is Already in Court — and Trans Awareness Week Starts Friday

Less than a week after Washington voters approved a ban on trans girls in girls' school sports, a coalition of parents, the state's teachers' union, and a trans family nonprofit sued to stop it before it ever takes effect — and as Transgender Awareness Week begins Friday, a free training course is opening up for the next generation of doctors.

November 11, 2026 · 6 min read

The ban didn't even get a week

Loves, we told you last week that Washington voters had approved Initiative 638, a ballot measure banning transgender girls from girls' school sports. It passed with about 56 percent of the vote on November 3, and it was scheduled to take effect on December 3. But on Monday, before the measure had even been in the news for a full week, it was already in court.

Parents of trans and cisgender girls, the nonprofit TransFamilies, and the Washington Education Association — the state's teachers' union, which represents more than 80,000 educators and school staff — filed a lawsuit in King County Superior Court asking a judge to declare Initiative 638 unconstitutional and to block it from taking effect. They are represented by the ACLU of Washington, Legal Voice, and Pacifica Law Group, and they have asked for emergency relief before the December 3 deadline.

Seattle Public Schools moved the same day: the district's board voted on Monday to authorize legal action of its own against the measure.

This is fast, and that speed matters. When a law is challenged before it takes effect, the question a judge answers first is not whether the law is good policy — it is whether it should be allowed to start hurting people while the case is argued. The coalition is asking for a preliminary injunction, which would freeze the ban in place while the full case plays out. If they win that first step, no girl in Washington would have to prove her sex to play school sports while the courts decide the bigger question.

What the lawsuit actually says

The complaint argues that Initiative 638 violates four separate provisions of the Washington Constitution. The core of the case is about privacy and equal treatment, and it is worth understanding in plain language, because the measure does not do what its supporters said it would do.

Initiative 638 does not just bar trans girls from girls' teams. To enforce that ban, it requires every girl who wants to play school sports to verify her sex assigned at birth — through hormone or genetic testing, or through an examination of her reproductive anatomy. Boys do not have to do any of that. The lawsuit argues that this one-sided verification regime violates the state constitution's privacy protections and its Equal Rights Amendment, which forbids discrimination on the basis of sex, because it subjects girls — cisgender and trans alike — to intrusive medical checks that boys never face.

"This is a fabricated issue being manufactured to create alarm," said Tiffani Lennon, executive director of Legal Voice, in the announcement of the suit. And the coalition's argument is not only about trans girls: parents of cisgender girls are among the plaintiffs, too, because their daughters would also be required to submit to testing just to join a team.

Adrien Leavitt, a staff attorney with the ACLU of Washington, put the human side of it plainly: sports give young people "a place to make friends and be part of a team," and the initiative "seeks to deny transgender girls those opportunities simply because of who they are."

The measure was pushed by Let's Go Washington, a political action committee sponsored by Redmond hedge-fund manager Brian Heywood. It was the only one of three Heywood-backed measures to pass this year — and now it may never be implemented at all. The groups behind the lawsuit say the case could take up to two years to fully resolve, but the first decision — whether the ban is frozen before December 3 — could come within weeks.

Why this fight is about health, not just sports

You might be wondering why a sports ban leads a trans health blog. Here is why: school sports are health care, in the broadest sense of the word. For trans kids, who already face higher rates of isolation and worse mental health outcomes when they are excluded, a team is often the one place in a school day where they belong without having to explain themselves. Taking that away is not a neutral administrative rule. It is a health decision made by people who will never have to live with it.

And the verification regime is a health issue in its own right. A law that requires a teenage girl to produce hormone test results or submit to an examination of her reproductive anatomy just to play volleyball is a law that puts children's bodies under state inspection. That is the part of this measure that affects every girl in Washington, trans or not — and it is the part that should alarm every parent, whatever they think about the sports question itself.

There is also a pattern worth naming. Washington is a state where trans people have been protected by law for years, where trans candidates just won seats in the legislature, and where voters still approved this measure by twelve points. The lesson is not that Washington turned against trans people overnight. The lesson is that a well-funded campaign can sell a ballot measure on fear even in a state that mostly supports trans rights — and that the courts, not the ballot box, are where the fight gets finished. That is exactly what happened this week.

Trans Awareness Week starts Friday — and the doctors of tomorrow are getting trained

Now for the hopeful half of today's news, because it arrives right on time. Transgender Awareness Week runs November 13 through 19, leading into Transgender Day of Remembrance on Friday, November 20. The week is observed every year to raise the visibility of trans and gender-nonconforming people, celebrate trans lives and achievements, and confront the discrimination trans people face. The Day of Remembrance, founded in 1999 by advocate Gwendolyn Ann Smith to honor Rita Hester, is a solemn day for remembering the trans people whose lives were taken by anti-trans violence in the past year.

And in the United Kingdom, the week is bringing something genuinely practical: medical training platform Affirm is offering a free, CPD-accredited online course on providing transgender healthcare, open to medical students at UK universities from November 13 to 20. Students can register with their university email address.

The course has six modules covering clinical pathways to gender-affirming care, gender norms, sexuality, the history of trans health, first-hand accounts from trans and nonbinary people, and the clinician's role. It is adapted from the book Gender Affirming Therapy: A Guide to What Transgender and Non-Binary Clients Can Teach Us, and it is endorsed by the trans healthcare provider Gender Plus.

Why does this matter? Because the gap it is filling is enormous. A 2026 review commissioned by NHS England found that LGBTQ+ health teaching is not a required part of UK medical training — and just one in eight medical students felt their knowledge of LGBTQ+ healthcare was good enough. That is the pipeline problem behind so many of the stories we cover on this blog: trans people who dread seeking care because they expect to be misunderstood, and doctors who worry about saying the wrong thing because nobody ever taught them. Affirm's co-founder, clinical psychologist Laura Scarrone Bonhomme, put it this way: "Doctors deserve to feel fully prepared for the realities of modern clinical practice."

One free course will not fix a training system. But every doctor who takes it is a doctor a trans patient will not have to educate from scratch — and that is how access actually improves, one clinician at a time.

Science note: hormones are still teaching researchers new things

One more piece of news from this week, for the science lovers among you. Researchers in Leipzig, Germany, are preparing the second phase of their Hormones and Health study, which follows trans people undergoing gender-affirming hormone therapy to learn how estrogen and testosterone affect the body — work that could improve care for trans patients and deepen everyone's understanding of sex hormones.

Around 40 trans people took part in the first phase, half trans men and half trans women, all examined before starting hormone therapy and at several points during treatment. The first findings, published in the journal Communications Medicine in 2026, looked at temperature perception in 12 trans women and 17 trans men. The researchers found that sensitivity to changes in warmth and cold increased in the trans women during treatment with estrogen and a testosterone blocker — a shift roughly equivalent to the difference previously observed between cis women and cis men. The study's leader, Dr. Haiko Schlögl, called it the first evidence that gender-affirming hormone therapy causes the skin's temperature-sensing system to adapt to the new "hormonal sex."

The second cohort, expected to begin in early 2027 and run for two years, will look closer at metabolism, body composition, energy expenditure, blood-vessel function, and even the brain. It is a reminder of something the loudest voices in the care debate like to ignore: the science of trans health is still being written, by researchers who are actually studying trans people — and the evidence keeps pointing in the direction of care that works.

What to watch next

Three dates to keep in your pocket. First, December 3: the day Initiative 638 would take effect unless a judge blocks it — watch for a ruling on the preliminary injunction before then. Second, Friday, November 13: Transgender Awareness Week begins, and with it a week of events, vigils, and conversations in communities everywhere. Third, Friday, November 20: Transgender Day of Remembrance, when we pause and say the names of the trans people we lost this year.

Today's news holds both halves of the story we keep telling on this blog. A ban passed, and the people it targets fought back within days — with parents, teachers, and lawyers standing beside them. And a week devoted to trans visibility is about to begin, with a new generation of doctors getting the training the last generation never had. The fight is real, loves, and so is the progress. Hold both.

What you can do next: If you live in Washington, follow the Initiative 638 case — a preliminary injunction ruling is expected before December 3. If you are a UK medical student, the Affirm course is free from November 13 to 20 and you can register with your university email. And as Transgender Awareness Week begins Friday, check for a local vigil or event for Transgender Day of Remembrance on November 20 — showing up is one of the simplest, most powerful things an ally can do.
